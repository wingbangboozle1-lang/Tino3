Phased entry after a sale, tested on past crashes

A mixed path: sell, park the cash in Treasuries, then buy in stages. Tested on the 1929 and 2007 falls.

What this page tests

The phased path is a mixed path: sell, park the proceeds in short Treasuries for a fixed period, then buy stocks in twelve equal monthly steps. It is a rule chosen before the test and not tuned to the results. Each test compares it with a lump-sum purchase on the same entry month, using the same index path.

The value of the crash test is mainly the downside. Its limit is that it is run on a few historical episodes, and the sensitivity table shows how much the answer moves with the entry month.

Headline, with its reinvestment risk beside it

  • 1929 crash, September entry. The lowest point of a lump sum was −87.1% against its entry value. The lowest point of the 24-month phased rule was −27.3%, and of the 30-month rule −2.1%. Value after 96 months: lump sum $442,250, 24-month phased $2,398,430 (per $1,000,000 of proceeds).
  • 2007 crash, October entry. The lowest point of a lump sum was −48.5% against its entry value. The 24-month phased rule never fell below its entry value.
  • The cost, in a market that did not fall. In the January 2012 control, the 24-month phased rule ended −37.0% against a lump sum over 96 months.
  • Reinvestment risk. The cash earned far less than its entry yield while it was parked (table below). The phased results above already include the actual parking yields, so the risk is in the numbers, not added on top.

In short: phasing reduces the depth of a fall that occurs during the wait. It costs return when the market rises. It is a trade-off between the downside and the upside, not a free improvement.

The test uses monthly averages because the monthly series is the only one that spans 1929 to 1939 in the sources read. A monthly average cannot show the intra-month fall, so the 1929 drawdown is understated.

What this test is not, and what it leaves out

  • Index, not portfolio. Dividends are excluded for both strategies, and no fees, taxes or transaction costs are charged. The two strategies are comparable with each other, not with a total-return figure.
  • Monthly averages. The 1929 episode uses monthly averages; the 2007 episode uses monthly averages of the NASDAQ Composite, a technology-weighted proxy, because the S&P 500 and Dow monthly series available to this build began in December 2016. A broad-index series for 2007 to 2010 has not been checked.
  • Parking leg. For 1929 there is no 2-year Treasury series in the sources read, so the 3-to-6-month series is used. For 2007 both the 3-month bill and the 2-year note are tested.
  • Normalised amount. Results are per $1,000,000 of proceeds. That is a unit for comparison, not the owner's inherited amount, which is not published.
  • Not in the decision model yet. The five-path comparison on the decision page does not yet include this mixed path; this page reports it alongside those results and does not change them.

How the numbers on this page are produced

Every computed figure on this page comes from scripts/model.py, which reads assets/data/inputs.json and assets/data/series.json. Monetary results are rounded to the nearest $10 before they are printed. The rules the model implements are the published official rules below; where a page uses a stated assumption or an unverified owner figure, that row says so and is logged on the register of unverified and assumed items.

  • California Legislative Information - Revenue and Taxation Code section 63.2
  • California State Board of Equalization - Publication 29, Property Taxpayers' Guide
  • Internal Revenue Service - Gifts and inheritances - inherited property basis and sale
  • Internal Revenue Service - Topic no. 409, Capital gains and losses
