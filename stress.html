Growth and inflation are never constant

Forty ten-year windows from the published record and twelve named shocks, replayed on the same four options.

The recommendation holds in most conditions, but it is not unconditional. Selling ends ahead in 32 of the 40 ten-year windows in the published record and in 10 of the 12 shocks tested. It loses when inflation runs far above the 2% property-tax cap, and when house prices compound at 8% a year or more.

The base case applies one growth rate and one inflation rate for ten years. The record does not behave that way, and neither will the future. Every window below replays the whole decision - property tax reset, Proposition 19 amount, rent cap, income tax, selling costs - on the market path that actually occurred. Renting ends ahead in 8 of 40 windows; moving in never does. A stock crash does not move the answer towards the house: it moves it towards Treasuries.

Why growth and inflation are never constant

A 50-year projection that applies one growth rate and one inflation rate describes an average, not a life. The published record shows how far the averages hide: house prices in Area A rose 11.6% a year for the ten years from 1996 and 1.1% a year for the ten years from 2001. Consumer price inflation over ten-year windows in the same record ran between 1.6% and 6.8% a year, and the ten-year Treasury averaged between 2.0% and 10.7%. Those swings are large enough to decide this question on their own, which is why the stress tests below are run on the actual sequences rather than on averages.

One structural point matters more than any single shock. The property tax resets when the home is inherited, and after that the assessed value is capped: it may rise no faster than the lower of 2% and the change in the California consumer price index, and it may never exceed market value.

Only two shocks change which option is ahead. If house prices rise 8% a year, renting finishes $561,790 ahead of selling and reinvesting: at that rate the home outruns an index fund. If the stock market falls 35% in the first year, Treasuries finish $636,880 ahead of the index fund — but selling into Treasuries still beats keeping the home by $98,030.

The shocks that hurt the home most are the ones with no inflation to offset them: flat or falling prices, deflation, a rent squeeze, and a five-fold rise in insurance. Each of those widens the gap against keeping the house.

What this means for the decision

  1. The base case is not the reason to sell. The reason is that the house has to beat a diversified portfolio while carrying a reset property tax, a single-building concentration, illiquidity, and maintenance. On the record it manages that only in high-inflation decades or in decades when house prices rise well ahead of everything else.
  2. Inflation is the one condition that consistently favours keeping the house, because the tax cap hands the owner part of the inflation and the mortgage-free house is a real asset. If the family's own view is that the next twenty years look like 1973-1982, keeping and renting is defensible. That is a view, not a projection.
  3. A stock crash does not make the house the safer choice. The 1999 start is the case to look at: an index fund bought at the peak finished last of the four, and the answer that beat it was renting, not moving in.
  4. Sequence risk cuts both ways. The ten years after the inheritance are what matters, and no projection can know them. That is an argument for not making an irreversible decision on a single point estimate: sell is reversible, moving in is not.
