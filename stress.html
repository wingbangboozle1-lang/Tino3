Economic stress cases — black swans and irregular 50-year paths

Official historical episodes and a private method for testing a 50-year horizon where growth and inflation are never even, instead of using one constant rate.

History is not a forecast. These episodes are anchors for stress testing, not return assumptions and not predictions for either property. The figures below are historical index/economic changes, not property-specific inputs.

Why a smooth 50-year line misleads

A constant-rate projection (for example, a steady 4% home-price and 2.5% inflation line for 50 years) implies a stability that long economic history does not show. Real decades contain booms, flat years and sharp falls in sequence. Use the episodes below and the private irregular-path method after them to mimic that unevenness rather than averaging it away.

Private method: run a 50-year horizon year by year with irregular paths

Build the private workbook with annual rows from the inheritance year to 50 years later. For each year record market value, assessed value (capped), property tax, insurance, utilities, maintenance, rent and net — but keep the figures private. Then test the same horizon three or more ways:

  1. A baseline with documented, sourced assumptions — clearly labeled as an illustration, not a forecast.
  2. Two or more historical replays: take a real 10- to 20-year official stretch and repeat its year-by-year pattern.
  3. One or more random resamplings of official annual changes to produce many irregular 50-year paths and a distribution, not a single line.
  4. At least one adverse combination: low or negative home-price years, high-inflation years, a vacancy year, a major repair year and a law change year together.

See Projection method for the annual structure and Running costs for the line items. Do not publish the private values or treat any single path as a prediction.

Stress the whole decision, not only asset growth

  • Property A: falling market value, delayed sale, reassessment, insurance nonrenewal, major repair and a change in the planned occupancy date.
  • Property B: rent interruption, tenant turnover, lawful rent limits, owner-occupancy change, higher service costs and repair concentration.
  • Shared household: inflation spikes, deflation, job/income interruption, interest-rate shifts, market drawdowns and the need for liquidity.
  • Law: an exemption, tax threshold, rent rule or local ordinance changes before the decision or holding period ends.

Use separate private scenarios and show the source for each factual anchor. Do not project the historical return directly onto a specific house, rent stream or investment account.
