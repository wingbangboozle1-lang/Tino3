Growth and inflation are never that even

The official annual record from 1977 to 2025, replayed and resampled over fifty years, so the plan rests on real sequences rather than one constant rate.

A single growth rate is not a forecast. Every other page of this edition projects with one constant rate: 4% house prices, 2.5% inflation, 7% stocks, 4.88% Treasuries. This page replaces that with the year-by-year record: 49 years of official annual data (1977–2025) read from each publisher on 12 November 2026, replayed and resampled over fifty years. The baseline here reconciles with the decision page to the dollar.

The baseline is the weakest assumption in the edition

Each row below is one official annual series, taken as published. Read the range and the number of falling years, not the average. House prices in the area of Property A fell in 11 of 49 years and moved by as much as 30.2% up and 12.3% down in a single year. Consumer prices fell in 1 year. A constant 4% and a constant 2.5% sit inside that record only as averages; no single year looked like them.

What this changes and what it does not. Property A's market value in 2076 has a median of $64,475,290 against $22,030,720 on the constant path, and a 5th-to-95th range of $22,698,760 to $175,690,910. The tax bill is far tighter, because the 2% cap, not the market, sets it. That is the central point of this whole page: the house price is a wide distribution and the property tax is not. The sell decision is the one that carries the market risk; keeping the home converts a wide market outcome into a narrow tax outcome plus a wide maintenance and rent outcome.

Re-running the decision on all 2,000 draws, selling into the index fund is the best of the four options in 1,529 draws (76.4%), Treasuries in 193 (9.7%), renting Property A in 278 (13.9%) and moving in in 0. The margin of the sale over the better of rent and moving in has a median of $3,415,740, a 5th percentile of −$4,597,660 and a 95th percentile of $12,960,090; it is negative in 19.3% of draws. So the sale is the better bet, not a certainty, and the size of the bet is stated rather than hidden.

When the tax follows the market down

State law assesses real property at the lesser of the factored base year value and the full cash value on the lien date. The constant model uses only the first half of that sentence. Two facts follow, and they point in opposite directions:

  • Before a death the gap is enormous. Property A's taxable value is $550,540 against a market value of $3,100,000. Market value would have to fall 82.2% before the rule lowered the bill at all.
  • At the death the gap closes, because the taxable value is reset to market value. From that moment any fall in market value lowers the taxable value, so the rule binds on small falls, not only on crashes.

What the counter-case shows. Two of the four adversarial shapes reverse the recommendation and both reverse it towards renting Property A, not towards moving in: a repeating stagflation makes Treasuries the best of the two sale vehicles and ends with renting Property A ahead of both, and a permanent deflation with falling house prices makes renting the outright winner by a wide margin. Moving in is the best option in none of them. So the decision rule the evidence supports is narrower than "sell": sell into a real-return asset unless inflation runs above the yield available, in which case keep a leveraged, capped-tax asset instead. That is the condition the suggestions page asks to have decided in advance.
