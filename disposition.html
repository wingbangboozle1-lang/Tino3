Selling timing and the like-kind exchange

What waiting to sell costs, month by month, and what a like-kind exchange would defer.

The same-year sale, and what waiting costs

Property acquired from a decedent takes a basis equal to its value on the date of the decedent's death, and that is so whether or not an estate tax return is filed. The value on the alternate valuation date applies only if the executor files a return and elects it there. So a sale made at the value at death realises no gain and pays no capital gains tax: on a 2045 inheritance this model puts the tax on an immediate sale at $0.

That is the number every other path has to beat, and the table below asks the only question left: what does it cost to wait? Waiting buys appreciation and pays for it three times over - in selling costs on the higher price, in property tax on a reassessed value, and in a lost year of compounding. By the 2045 inheritance, twelve months of waiting costs $1,509,990 at the thirty-year horizon, and it still creates no taxable gain at all, because the 5.5% of selling costs is greater than a year of appreciation. The first rung that produces tax is 18 months.

What the ladder says

  • A sale in the same tax year as the inheritance is already the baseline sell path on the decision page. It is not a new path: it is the path, and this page states its tax explicitly instead of leaving it implicit.
  • Tax is not the reason to hurry. The first 18 months create no taxable gain at all, and the tax at sixty months is $362,830 on a price of $7,946,240 - about 4.57% of it.
  • Compounding is the reason to hurry. Almost the whole cost of waiting is the year of investment return given up, not the tax. That is why the cost per year of waiting is nearly constant across the ladder: $1,509,990 for the first year, $1,662,550 a year averaged over five.
  • None of this is a forecast of price. The house grows at the modelled 4.0% a year in every row. If the heir has a reason to think a specific sale date will fetch more, that is a view about price, and the black swan page is where the downside of acting on it is priced.

The like-kind exchange, priced the way the statute works

An exchange under section 1031 defers the tax a sale would realise. It does not forgive it: the deferred gain rides in the replacement property, whose basis is set by reference to the basis of the property given up, and it is taxed when the replacement is disposed of. It also does nothing about property tax - a transfer in an exchange is a change in ownership under Revenue and Taxation Code section 60, so the replacement is enrolled at its own value.

The finding, stated plainly: at the inheritance there is nothing to defer. The gain on a sale at the date-of-death value is $0, so the tax an exchange could postpone is $0. The replacement property would have to earn 6.30% a year - exactly the modelled equity return - for the exchange to break even. The step-up at death removes the reason most exchangers do this.

An exchange is a transfer to a third party, and section 63.2 ends the claim period on a transfer to a third party or when the transferee stops occupying. The replacement is enrolled at its own full value under section 60. The excluded roll grows at the 2% cap while a reassessed value grows with the market, so the gap widens every year, which is why the cumulative column is not simply the annual column multiplied by the years left.

The two reliefs compete for the same two years

The property-tax relief and the exchange compete for the same years. Claiming the intergenerational exclusion means making the home the principal residence; qualifying a dwelling unit for an exchange means renting it at a fair rental and keeping personal use to the greater of 14 days or 10% of the rental days in each of two 12-month periods. A household cannot do both with the same two years, and a home used solely as a personal residence does not qualify at all.

So the sequence the statute allows is: either move in, claim the exclusion, and keep it by staying, or let the property at a fair rental for two years with personal use kept to the safe harbour's limits, and only then exchange. A household that wants the property-tax relief and the deferral at the same time has to choose which one it is giving up, and on these figures the property-tax relief is worth $22,260 a year from the first year, against a deferral of $0 at the inheritance.

What the exchange costs that no column above can price

  • A qualified intermediary, exchange documents and legal review, none of which is quoted here because no quote exists for this household.
  • The 45-day and 180-day clocks run from the transfer of the relinquished property and do not stop for a market in which nothing suitable is available. If the deadlines are missed the exchange fails and the transfer is a sale, with the tax due in that year.
  • The replacement must be of like kind and in the United States: real property in the United States and real property outside it are not like kind (26 U.S.C. 1031(h)).
  • Exchanging keeps the capital in one asset, in one market, with the management that comes with it - the opposite of what the sell-and-diversify path buys.
  • The commission and other costs of the transfer out are not deductible and are not added to the replacement's basis: section 1031(d) sets that basis by reference to the basis of the property given up, so the costs are paid with no tax offset.
  • An exchange between related parties carries a two-year look-forward: if either side disposes within two years, the deferral is unwound (26 U.S.C. 1031(f)).

What this page cannot tell you

  • Whether the heir wants to be a landlord of a different building. The whole exchange question presupposes it. Nothing here recommends it.
  • Whether this property would be treated as held for investment rather than for sale. Section 1031(a)(2) excludes real property held primarily for sale, and the Internal Revenue Service's own safe harbour procedures record that a personal residence does not qualify and that the mere hope of selling at a gain is not an investment intent. That is a facts question for the attorney, not a number.
  • What the replacement property would be, or earn. No replacement is assumed. The break-even columns are published instead of a forecast, which is the honest way round: the site does not know the return, so it publishes the return the property would need.
  • Whether the transfer itself is excluded from reassessment. Every property tax figure here assumes the intergenerational exclusion exists at all, which depends on the relationship question A-1.
  • Whether a qualified intermediary's fee, legal costs or the exchange documents change the arithmetic. They are not priced, because no quote exists.
