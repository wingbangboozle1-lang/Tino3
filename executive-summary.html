Executive summary

The important details, the recommendation, and what would change it.

First gate: is the transfer between a parent and a child?

Every reassessment figure on this site assumes the transfer will qualify for the intergenerational exclusion in Revenue and Taxation Code section 63.2. That section excludes a transfer only where it is between parents and their children, or between grandparents and grandchildren where every parent of the grandchild who is a child of the grandparent has died. “Children” is defined in the statute to include a stepchild, a son-in-law or daughter-in-law, and an adopted child adopted before age 18; it also includes a foster child of a licensed foster parent where adoption was barred. The relationship is a condition of the exclusion, not a detail: if the transferor and the heir are not in one of those relationships, no exclusion is available, the property is reassessed to full market value, and the entire saving shown on this page disappears.

This analysis is written on the assumption that it is. The trust document is the only thing that confirms it, and this site has not seen it. Until it is confirmed, treat every Proposition 19 shelter figure here as conditional. See question A-1.

Recommendation

Sell Property A soon after it is inherited, unless the heir genuinely wants to live in that house.

Selling converts an asset earning nothing into capital, at almost no tax, and it removes a property tax bill that is about to multiply by six to eleven times. Living in the house is the only path that keeps the tax bill lower, and it still leaves the heir holding a single, undiversified, illiquid asset in one California school district. Letting it out fails every scenario this site can build, because it combines the full reassessment with landlord costs and a rental yield below the return available elsewhere — but that comparison credits the letting paths with no rent at all, so read it as a floor and check the break-even rent below before relying on it.

The counter-case is real and it is on the black swan page: keeping the house wins in 2 of the 6 stress regimes, namely Great Inflation repeat; A fast inflation burst then a rate shock. That is a bet on one neighbourhood, not a plan.

Five things that could change the answer

  1. The heir wants to live there. That is worth the whole gap if it is true. It also has to be true for the whole time: moving out removes the Proposition 19 exclusion.
  2. Law changes. On unofficial results as of 16 November 2026 (7:12 p.m.; counting continues), California voters approved Proposition 3, which makes the high-income tax rates permanent (60.8% yes), and rejected Proposition 40, the one-time billionaire tax (52.2% no). Certification is due by 11 December 2026. Both are on the law page. A future measure could cut the other way.
  3. The house outgrows everything. See the stress cases.
  4. The estate is large enough to matter federally. See the estate page: the 2026 exclusion is high, but it is indexed and can be cut by Congress.
  5. An input is wrong. Every owner-supplied figure is flagged. The questions page is the list of what to confirm and how.

How the numbers on this page are produced

Every computed figure on this page comes from scripts/model.py, which reads assets/data/inputs.json and assets/data/series.json. Monetary results are rounded to the nearest $10 before they are printed. The rules the model implements are the published official rules below; where a page uses a stated assumption or an unverified owner figure, that row says so and is logged on the register of unverified and assumed items.
