The plan in order

  1. Now. Confirm the trust is revocable and holds title; confirm the deed matches the owner's statement that Property B is already owned by the beneficiary; confirm which home carries the parents' homeowners' exemption. Do not move either property into an LLC.
  2. At the inheritance. Get a date-of-death appraisal. File the change in ownership statement within 150 days. File Form 706 within nine months if the estate is near the exclusion or a spouse survives.
  3. Decide within the first year. Sell, unless living in the home is worth about $8,580 a month in today's dollars to the family. If moving in, occupy within one year and file both claims.
  4. Reinvest. A stock index fund has the higher expected result. Treasuries at today's yield finish ahead of renting when house prices grow 4% a year or less. A mix sits between the two.
  5. Keep Property B. Its low assessed value and steady rent make it the better property to hold, provided it is not reassessed in the same estate.

Before acting: answer, choose, then implement

  1. Answer the gating questions. The law settles ten of them on the questions page (QA-01 to QA-10). Nine documents settle the rest without publishing anything private: the deed, the trust, the itemised bills, two quotes per property, the estate inventory, the solar letter, a rent survey, the occupancy record and, at the death, an appraisal (D-01 to D-09).
  2. Choose a path. The suggestions page sets out four: sell and reinvest (P1, recommended), rent Property A (P2), move in (P3), or defer (P4, not modelled). Nothing is implemented until the choice is recorded.
  3. Pass the gates. Evidence, privacy, arithmetic, the owner's recorded choice, then publication. A row without an official link fails the build; so does a name, address, postal code or parcel number.
  4. Re-run, do not edit. A change means editing the model, re-running it, and comparing the new result with the old one before anything is published.
