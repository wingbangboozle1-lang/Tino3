Executive summary

The whole case on one page: what the law actually says, what the numbers actually are, what to do, and what could change the answer.

The recommendation

Sell Property A on inheritance and reinvest the net proceeds; do not make Property A your home and do not treat renting it as the default. At a 2045 inheritance the after-tax position twenty years later is $10,585,440 from selling into Treasuries, $8,134,340 from renting at an indicative $16,650/month, and $5,561,810 from living in it. The ordering holds at 2%, 3% and 4% appreciation. Renting only wins if you clear $31,650/month and values compound near 5.4%; living in it only wins if you value the address at roughly $251,180 a year.

What to do next

Three actions, in this order, and none of them cost anything yet.

  1. Get the estate inventory, in writing. Total value of everything in the trust — both houses, cash, brokerage, retirement accounts — as of the most recent statement. Without it, the $15,000,000 federal test cannot be run. Expected result: the two houses alone leave $10,100,000 of headroom in 2026, but the cash is the unknown.
  2. Ask the trustee which house is the transferor's principal residence. That single fact decides whether the $1,044,586 Prop 19 exclusion is available on Property B, which is worth roughly $641,170 of taxable value in the first year alone.
  3. Order two opinions in the inheritance year: a date-of-death appraisal (it is your basis and the assessor's new value) and, if you are considering renting, two written rental appraisals. Then pick the path within twelve months of death.
