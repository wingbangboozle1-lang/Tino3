# Executive summary

Built 16 November 2026 from the same deterministic model as the site. Every dollar figure is rounded to the nearest $10. Anonymised throughout: **Property A** is the single-family home being inherited through a trust; **Property B** is the three-unit building the heir already owns. No name, street address, postal parcel number, postal code, county or city name, trust term or utility account appears here or on the site; the county and city are deliberately withheld because they identify the properties.

Planning analysis, not legal, tax or investment advice.

## The recommendation

**Sell Property A soon after it is inherited, unless the heir genuinely wants to live in that house.** Selling leads in the baseline, in 9 of the 9 sensitivity combinations and in 4 of the 6 stress regimes, and it is the only path that does not depend on the heir wanting that house, being able to hold it as a principal residence, or finding a tenant at an implausible rent.

## One condition outranks every number below

The intergenerational exclusion in Revenue and Taxation Code section 63.2 applies **only** where the transfer is between a parent and a child, or between a grandparent and a grandchild whose parent has died. That is a condition of the exclusion, not a detail. If the relationship is different, no exclusion is available, the property is reassessed to full market value, every shelter figure below disappears, and the case for selling gets stronger. The trust document is the only thing that settles it and this analysis has not seen it. **Question A-1 on the questions page.**

## How often the answer holds

- Selling beats living in it for thirty years: **92.8%** of 2000 resampled histories.
- Selling beats living in it ten years and then selling: **95.5%** of 2000 resampled histories.
- Selling beats living ten years and then letting it out: **94.8%** of 2000 resampled histories.
- Draws with falling nominal house prices over the thirty years: **0.2%** of 2000 resampled histories.

The draws are ten-year blocks of the verified California house price index and consumer price index, drawn together so house and inflation history move as they did. The equity return is held at the published scenario, because no official series of forward equity returns exists.

## Corrections in this edition

- The combined depreciation recapture rate is corrected from 41.1% to **42.1%**. Its own stated components are 25% federal, 13.3% California and 3.8% net investment income tax, which sum to 42.1. Every figure that depends on recapture was rebuilt.
- The transfer tax and recording fees on the sell path are itemised: about $5,520 on a $5,000,000 sale, about 2.0% of the modelled 5.5% selling cost. The $75 instrument fee does **not** apply, because Government Code section 27388.1(a)(2)(A) exempts an instrument recorded in connection with a transfer subject to the documentary transfer tax.
- The initiative to repeal most of Proposition 19 **failed to qualify** for the November 2026 ballot (Secretary of State memorandum, 18 May 2026), so the shelter arithmetic is current law rather than law about to change. It is recorded as dead on the law page.
- **The recommendation did not move.** Three things were added to the model in this edition and none of them changed which path leads: keeping the home and borrowing against its equity, upkeep inflating at the rate the official record actually shows, and Property B treated as three units rather than one income figure. Selling still leads the baseline, the sensitivity grid and the stress regimes.
- **Keep and borrow, priced as a path.** Borrowing half the value of a $6,531,230 house - $3,265,620 - at the published 7.11% mortgage average, on a 30-year level-payment schedule with $32,660 of charges, costs $21,970 a month and ends $3,559,140 after thirty years, against $5,322,760 for simply living there with no loan and $38,585,540 for selling: worse than both. The loan costs more than the money it raises earns after tax, so the carry is negative by 0.81 percentage points before fees, and no deduction helps because Publication 936 allows one only where the borrowed money buys, builds or substantially improves the home that secures it. Creating the deed of trust is itself not a change in ownership under Revenue and Taxation Code section 62, so the property tax stays the living path's. Only the low end of the published rate band (5.98%) beats the unleveraged keep, by up to $1,069,000, and that is a rate 1.13 points below the one published this month rather than a return the household can choose.
- **Upkeep, checked against the official record.** The model inflates maintenance at 2.5% a year. The federal household-outlay series for maintenance, repairs and other expenses of an owned dwelling grew 7.74% a year from 2015 to 2024, against 3.16% for consumer prices over the same window, and the allowance charged here for the owner-occupied house is 3.8 times the published national average of REDACTED-FIGURE. Charging upkeep at the measured rate moves the thirty-year living path from $5,322,760 to $1,842,100 and, if it is charged on each year's house value rather than on the value at death, to -$4,809,860 - which is the one result here that turns the keep case negative on its own. The series bundles insurance, is a national mean, and stops at 2024, so it is a cross-check and never an input. Selling still leads every version of it.

## The numbers that drive it

- **The tax cliff.** The Property A bill is about $7,360 today. If the heir inherits in 2045 and does not move in, it becomes about $79,330. If the heir moves in and claims the exclusion, it becomes about $57,060.
- **The shelter is smaller than the growth.** On a 2045 inheritance the exclusion shelters the old taxable value plus $1,886,640 against a market value of $6,531,230. Everything above the sum is reassessed.
- **The tax-free window.** Property acquired from a decedent takes a basis equal to its value at death, so a sale soon afterwards pays about zero capital gains tax. In the 2045 baseline a $6,531,230 sale carries $0 of tax.
- **What waiting costs.** Hold the same house thirty years and the tax on the eventual sale is about $4,910,950 after the section 121 exclusion; let it out as well and depreciation recapture pushes that to about $7,203,420.
- **The break-even.** Keeping the home beats selling only if it appreciates 8.66% a year for thirty years. The verified California state house-price index compounded at 6.4% a year from 1975 to 2025 and 2.6% a year over the latest twenty years in the record ([FRED CASTHPI](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CASTHPI)).
- **The break-even rent.** Letting would need about $30,220 a month, $362,600 a year, in 2045, to match selling and investing.

## The black swans, and what they do to the answer

Real economies do not grow at a constant rate. In the verified record used here, annual consumer price inflation ranged from -0.3% to 13.5%, and annual house-price growth in the California state-level index ranged from -20.0% to 25.3% ([FRED CASTHPI](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CASTHPI)). CPI figures use [FRED CPIAUCSL](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CPIAUCSL). The regimes below replay the worst stretches of that record against a 2045 inheritance over thirty years.

| Regime | Inflation | House growth | Treasury | Best path |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Great Inflation repeat | 9.8% | 17.2% | 10.13% | Live in it |
| A long house price slump | 3.4% | -0.6% | 7.06% | Sell, reinvest in Treasuries |
| A financial crisis crash | 2.2% | -9.4% | 3.51% | Sell, invest in equities |
| A fast inflation burst then a rate shock | 5.6% | 10.1% | 2.78% | Live in it |
| House prices flat for the whole horizon | 2.5% | 0.0% | 4.7% | Sell, invest in equities |
| Low inflation and falling nominal house prices | 0.5% | -1.0% | 2.0% | Sell, invest in equities |

Sold into equities or Treasuries wins 4 of the 6 regimes. Keeping the house wins only in: Great Inflation repeat; A fast inflation burst then a rate shock. These are scenario results, not forecasts for a particular neighborhood.

## Estate and inheritance tax

- California has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. No California estate tax return is required for a death on or after 1 January 2005, and an inheritance is not California income.
- The federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person for 2026 and $15,480,000 for 2027. Portability lets a surviving spouse use a deceased spouse's unused exclusion if a timely return is filed.
- Above the exclusion the federal rate reaches 40%. The tax is on the estate, not on the heir, and is due with Form 706 nine months after death, with an extension available.
- The cash inheritance is deliberately not published and not modelled as a figure; the estate page works in anonymous scenario bands instead.

### If the estate does exceed the exclusion, the tax is due in cash on a fixed date

- Federal estate tax is 40% of the amount above the exclusion and is due 9 months after the date of death. A six-month extension of time to file the return is not an extension of time to pay.
- Interest on anything unpaid runs from the original due date at the federal short-term rate plus three percentage points, compounded daily. That rate was 7% in the fourth quarter of 2026 and has ranged from 3% to 8% across 2022 to 2026.
- On the $5,000,000 band, deferring five years costs $455,260 in interest against $456,790 of return the same cash would have earned, so deferral is close to break-even at five years and only clearly worthwhile at ten.
- The two-person case changes the estate side, not the recommendation: two exclusions with portability would shelter $48,287,040 in 2045 against $24,143,520 for one person. The joint section 121 exclusion is worth $92,750 at most, and nothing at all on a sale soon after inheritance.
- Full bands, the five routes to raising the cash, and the cost of borrowing instead of selling: the estate liquidity page. Whether the section 6166 instalment election is available is a question for the attorney; this site does not assume it, because a home occupied as a personal residence is not an interest in a closely held business on the face of the statute.

## What is still open

These sit on the flagged-for-review page, each with the publisher that holds the record:

- The owner's market values, the property-tax and assessment histories, the rent and the utility and insurance estimates are the owner's own records, not checked against a county roll or a quote.
- Property A's tax rate area and its published ad valorem rate. 1.18% is assumed and flagged.
- A property-specific insurance premium for either building. The Department of Insurance publishes a statewide average written premium for each policy form - the newest year read gives $1,074 for an owner-occupied homeowners form and $519 for a rental dwelling or landlord form on up to four units - and an online premium survey, not an average for a postal area. Both buildings are now carried as bands bounded by those figures instead of one shared estimate, and the whole band moves the thirty-year living figure by under 1% of the gap to selling, so it is a bill to be sized rather than a decision to be made.
- Whether either building carries a mortgage, deed of trust or lien at the transfer. Nothing in the record says either way, so both are modelled free and clear and the decision page states that as an assumption and prices each stated payment level. An encumbrance changes the size of every keeping path's bill and never the ranking, because a holder pays the interest as well as the principal. The reverse path - choosing to borrow against an unencumbered house instead of selling it - is now modelled in full and is dominated on every rung tested; what is still missing is a lender's and a trustee's answer, which is the difference between a scenario and an option.
- A market rent for Property A, and the seller's actual selling costs.
- Whether the trust actually holds title, and whether the transfer is between a parent and a child.

**Two opened in this pass, both worked and both published.** The living path is credited with no housing at all, so it is charged every cost of owning and credited with the value of nothing; credited at the market rent it would otherwise cost, living and selling come level at about 100% of that rent. And the fixed charges inside Property B's tax bill are 44.7% of the bill and grew 8.12% a year over its roll, against a 2.5% assumption, so the 2% cap cannot bound that bill the way the tables imply.
