Two things grow at different speeds and in opposite directions. Market value moves with the housing market; assessed value is pinned to the Proposition 13 ceiling of 2% a year and cannot exceed it absent a change in ownership. The gap between the two columns is the tax the heirs will eventually pay.

What the columns mean

Market value is a projection on an assumed growth rate, not a forecast and not a verified figure. Assessed value is the parents' Proposition 13 base, compounding at the statutory 2% cap (S17). Property tax is the owners' reported 2026 bill compounded at the same 2%. No row here is a valuation you can rely on — they exist to show the shape of the problem.

The inheritance-year question

The brief puts the inheritance somewhere between 2040 and 2050. Everything that matters scales with that date, because market value compounds and the Prop 13 base barely moves.

The allowance is doing all the work, and it is not guaranteed

The Prop 19 allowance is index-linked to the FHFA California house price index and adjusted every second February (S02). The model assumes it grows 3.5% a year, reaching $2,008,220 by 2045. If it instead tracked general inflation, the allowance would be materially smaller and every post-inheritance tax figure on this page would be higher. This is an assumption with a visible effect; it is listed as such on the flagged page.

What the model does not project

  • Refinancing or borrowing. Both houses are assumed owned outright.
  • Capital improvement. Nothing is added to either basis, and no work is assumed beyond the maintenance allowance.
  • Special assessments or Mello-Roos. Neither parcel is known to carry one, and neither is confirmed to be free of one.
  • Changes in the tax rate itself. Bond debt retires and new bonds are issued; the rate is held at its current level plus nothing.
  • Proposition 8 declines. If a property's market value falls below its factored base year value, the assessor may temporarily reduce the assessment. The model never assumes a decline, which overstates tax in any downturn.
