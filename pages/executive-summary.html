1. The situation

Two residential properties in the San Francisco Bay Area will pass to the next generation through a trust, most likely between 2040 and 2050:

  • Cupertino, ZIP 95014. A single-family home, estimated at $3,100,000 in November 2026, assessed by Santa Clara County at $550,540 with a 2025-26 tax bill of $7,210. Rooftop solar.
  • San Francisco, Outer Sunset, ZIP 94122. A three-unit building, estimated at $1,800,000, assessed at $114,250 in 2025 with a tax bill of $2,440. Two units are let for a combined $7,150 a month; the third is vacant.

Alongside the real estate comes a cash inheritance large enough that estate-tax thresholds need to be watched. The analysis below is built to answer one question: live in one of them, rent them out, or sell and invest?

A fact the analysis depends on, which the brief does not state

Proposition 19 relief attaches only to the property that was the transferor's principal residence. The brief does not say which house the parents live in. The base case assumes Cupertino. Every result is also run with the assumption reversed; the difference is shown in section 6 and on the decision analysis page. Confirming this single fact is worth more than any other input.

2. The law that governs the transfer

Property tax — Proposition 13 and Proposition 19

Proposition 13 does three things. It caps the general levy at 1% of full cash value — evidenced by the county rate books, each of which states “1% Maximum Tax Levy 1.00000” for every tax rate area (S09 S10). It limits annual assessed-value growth to 2% (S17). And it reassesses to market value only on a change in ownership or new construction. Inheritance is a change in ownership, so the default is a reassessment to market value.

Proposition 19 (S01), effective for transfers on or after 16 February 2021, provides the only relief available to a child. It applies to a principal residence, requires the child to make it their own principal residence within one year and claim the homeowners' exemption, and caps the protected amount at the parent's factored base year value plus an inflation-adjusted allowance — $1,044,586 for transfers between February 2025 and February 2027 (S02). Anything above that cap is added back to the taxable value.

Three consequences follow, and they drive the whole analysis:

  1. The rental building in San Francisco gets no relief at all. Prop 19 deleted the old parent-child exclusion for property other than a principal residence.
  2. Cupertino's cap is long exceeded. Its base is $550,540 against a value of $3,100,000 — a gap of $2,549,460, roughly five times the allowance. Moving in saves $26,290 a year but does not restore the old bill.
  3. The claim is not automatic. It is Form BOE-19-P, filed with the county assessor within three years of the transfer or before the property is sold.

Estate and inheritance tax

California imposes no estate tax and no inheritance tax. The state's inheritance and gift tax was repealed by Proposition 6 in June 1982, and the residual "pick-up" tax produces no revenue for decedents dying after 2004 (S06). A state estate tax would need a constitutional amendment approved by voters (S07).

The only death tax that can apply is federal. For 2026 the basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000 per person, up from $13,990,000 for 2025, with a 40% rate above it (S03). A married couple can shelter $30,000,000 if a portability election is made on a timely return.

Whether the $15,000,000 level holds through the 2040s depends on future legislation. The IRS publishes the figure one year at a time; it publishes no long-range schedule. The projection on this site indexes the verified 2026 figure at an assumed inflation rate and says so.

Indexed at 2.5% a year, the single-person exclusion reaches roughly $23,979,750 by 2045. The two houses alone are projected at $10,323,560 that year, so the estate can absorb about $13,656,190 of cash before a federal return is required. Full threshold table →

Capital gains — the step-up is the single largest tax lever

Property acquired from a decedent takes a basis equal to fair market value at the date of death (S18). The appreciation that accrued during the parents' ownership is never taxed. On these two houses, the growth between 2026 and 2045 alone is $5,423,560; taxed at the assumed 37.1% blended rate that is $2,012,140 of tax that does not arise, simply because you inherit rather than receive a gift.

This has a direct planning consequence: do not ask for the houses to be transferred during the parents' lifetime. A lifetime gift carries over the parents' basis and forfeits the step-up.

5. Recommendation

Plan F — live in the San Francisco building, sell Cupertino at inheritance, invest the proceeds

Terminal after-tax wealth in 2065: $20,063,390, against $16,080,090 for living in Cupertino and keeping both, and $14,789,040 for selling both and renting.

It ranks first in eight of the nine growth-and-return combinations tested, and second in the ninth.

Why it wins. The reasoning is not "San Francisco is a better market". It is that after reassessment the Cupertino house becomes a poor financial asset while the San Francisco building stays a good one:

  • Cupertino, inhabited, costs $59,120 a year in property tax on a house that produces no income. Rented, it yields 0.5% net on value.
  • The San Francisco building yields 2.0% net after its own reassessment, because two of its three units are long-standing tenancies whose rent stream is real cash, and because a three-unit building worth less than the Cupertino house can carry rent that is nearly as large.
  • Selling Cupertino immediately after death costs almost nothing in capital gains — the step-up resets the basis to $6,531,230 — so the only frictions are about 5% in selling costs and $7,180 of county transfer tax.
  • You still need somewhere to live. Occupying the San Francisco building's vacant unit costs only the rent you forgo on that one unit, not market rent on a whole house.

What it is not. This is not a recommendation to be all-cash. Plan F leaves you with a $6,197,490 portfolio plus a rent-producing building — roughly 62% invested, 38% in Bay Area residential property.

7. What to do before 2040

  1. Establish, in writing, which house is the parents' principal residence. It is the assumption on which the entire property-tax analysis rests. A homeowners' exemption on the county roll is the cleanest evidence (S16).
  2. Do not take the houses as a lifetime gift. A gift forfeits the step-up in basis (S18) and would cost roughly $2,012,140 in avoidable capital-gains tax on the 2026–2045 appreciation alone.
  3. Review the trust instrument. Whether the trust or the beneficiary takes title, whether there is a sub-trust for the San Francisco building, and who is trustee all affect how quickly the deed can be recorded — and the deed is what starts the assessor's clock.
  4. Get real insurance quotes for both ZIP codes. This is the largest unresolved number in the cost model. See flagged item 4.
  5. Confirm the actual tax rate on each parcel. Both owner-supplied tax series imply effective rates that differ from the published rates. See flagged items 1 and 2.
  6. Decide the occupancy question now, on paper. If Plan F is the intention, the one-year move-in clock and the BOE-19-P filing are the two deadlines that cannot be missed. They are steps 12 and 15 of the inheritance process.
