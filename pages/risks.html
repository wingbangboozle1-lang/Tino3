A projection twenty years out is a statement about assumptions, not about the future. This page separates the risks that would change the numbers from the ones that would change the ranking, and records what is currently being proposed in Sacramento and Washington.

Law changes: what is actually happening

Proposition 19 repeal — failed, and not pending

A third attempt to repeal Prop 19's inheritance provisions was circulated as "Repeals voter-enacted changes to property tax rules for transfers between family members" (summary date 6 November 2025). On 18 May 2026 the Secretary of State recorded that the petition had failed: the number of signatures filed across all counties was below the requirement (S12).

Nothing is therefore pending. Prop 19 remains fully in force, and the analysis throughout this site uses current law. A future repeal would restore the pre-2021 rules, under which the parent's principal residence could pass with no value limit and no residency requirement — but it would not, by itself, restore the exclusion for the San Francisco rental, because the old Prop 58/193 regime for other real property was itself capped at $1,000,000 of factored base year value.

Ending the step-up in basis at the state level — proposed, not enacted

The Governor's 2024-25 budget package proposed ending the step-up in basis for inherited assets for California income tax purposes, for inheritances received after 1 July 2024. The Legislative Analyst's Office scored it as raising a few hundred million dollars a year (S08).

It was not enacted. It matters here more than any other proposal on this page: the step-up is worth roughly $2,012,140 on the 2026–2045 appreciation alone, and a state-level repeal would make Plan F materially less attractive than the model shows, because Plan F depends on selling at a stepped-up basis.

The federal estate tax

The 2025 federal law set the basic exclusion at $15,000,000 per person from 2026 and removed the scheduled sunset, indexing it for inflation thereafter (S03). "Permanent" here means only that it has no expiry date; a future Congress can change it. A reduction back toward half that level would put the projected 2045 estate — $10,323,560 in houses plus cash — within reach of a federal tax at 40%.

A California estate or wealth tax — not enacted

California imposes no estate, inheritance or wealth tax (S06 S07). Proposals for each have been introduced in earlier legislative sessions and none has been enacted; a state estate tax would require a constitutional amendment approved by the voters, because the Revenue and Taxation Code currently bars one. unverified — the status of any bill in the current session was not checked against the Legislature's bill database, so this entry is recorded as unverified rather than asserted.

Insurance market and the FAIR Plan

The FAIR Plan has grown sharply and has levied assessments on member insurers after major wildfire losses (S15). Two consequences for these properties: premiums should be expected to keep rising faster than inflation, and a property that is currently insurable in the admitted market may not stay that way. Neither house is in a high fire-hazard severity zone, which is protective, but a single repricing event can double a premium. The model escalates insurance at CPI plus two points, which may prove optimistic.

Rent control — with a live proposal

The San Francisco Rent Ordinance caps annual increases on the two sitting tenancies at 1.6% for 2026-27 (S13). There is also a specific proposal pending: on 9 November 2026 the Rent Board posted notice that the Board of Supervisors is considering File No. 261020, which would temporarily limit the combined amount of banked and capital-improvement increases that may be imposed on rent-controlled units to 10% per year. The notice itself states the legislation has not been enacted (S13).

Anything that further tightens rent control reduces the rent stream that makes the San Francisco building the better asset. That would weaken Plan F and strengthen the case for selling both.

The model assumes the two sitting tenancies escalate at 1.6% indefinitely. That is conservative: a vacant unit can be reset to market, and banked increases are available within the ordinance's rules.

What would make the recommendation wrong

  1. A sustained low return on capital. Below roughly 5% nominal on a diversified portfolio, keeping and renting Cupertino overtakes selling it. This is the only single-variable change in the tested range that reverses the ranking.
  2. A decision not to be a landlord at all. Then the choice is between D and E, and the differences among the remaining plans are small relative to the value of not having tenants.
  3. Information not in evidence. If the San Francisco building needs seismic or sewer work that has not been done, or if a tenancy is not in fact rent-controlled, the yield that justifies keeping it disappears.
