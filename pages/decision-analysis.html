Six plans, each run from 2045 to 2065 on identical assumptions. The only things that change are which house you occupy, which house you let, and which you sell. Everything else — growth, inflation, returns, tax rates — is held constant so the comparison is clean.

Method, in one paragraph

Each year, rent received is reduced by property tax, insurance, water, electricity, maintenance and income tax. Where you do not occupy an inherited house, market rent for an equivalent house is subtracted, so every plan buys the same standard of housing. The resulting annual cash flow is invested at 6.0% nominal; the 2% income component is taxed each year. At 2065 any remaining house is sold with 5% selling costs, documentary transfer tax, capital gains on the post-inheritance gain and depreciation recapture, and the portfolio is liquidated and taxed on unrealised gains. The result is a single number: after-tax wealth at the end of 2065.

A negative “invested portfolio” is not a debt: it is the cumulative amount of outside cash the plan requires, because property tax and running costs exceed the rent it collects. Plan A needs about $583,950 of outside funding over twenty years.

Why the ranking uses terminal wealth rather than a discounted present value

A present-value ranking depends entirely on the discount rate chosen, and this model already assumes the alternative is a portfolio earning 6.0%. Discounting at that same rate makes every plan's present value collapse into a restatement of its terminal wealth, so nothing is added. Discounting at a lower rate would silently assume you could borrow at that rate to fund the negative cash-flow years. Terminal after-tax wealth is the one number that needs no such choice, so it is the ranking metric throughout. The present values are still computed and stored in the model file for audit.

The same table, explained

A. Live in Cupertino, rent all three San Francisco units
The plan the brief implies. It preserves the Prop 19 exclusion on Cupertino — $26,290 a year of tax avoided — and it captures the full rent on the San Francisco building. But the combined property tax of $103,970 exceeds the net rent, so the plan runs a cash deficit of about $9,540 a year from day one.
B. Live in San Francisco, rent Cupertino
Cupertino loses its exclusion because you do not occupy it, so its tax rises by $26,290. In exchange, Cupertino produces rent and you occupy a unit in a building you already own. This is the best plan if the portfolio return turns out low, because it keeps both houses appreciating.
C. Rent both, rent somewhere else
Worst of the six. You pay market rent for housing, lose the exclusion on both houses, and pay tax on both rent streams. Renting equivalent housing at a 3% gross yield is structurally worse than owning it.
D. Sell both, invest, rent
Cleanest balance sheet and the lowest concentration risk, but it ranks fifth. The reason is not tax — selling at death is nearly tax-free — it is that you convert two appreciating assets into a taxable portfolio while taking on a rent liability that grows every year.
E. Live in Cupertino, sell San Francisco
Gives up the highest-yielding asset in the portfolio. Fourth of six.
F. Live in San Francisco, sell Cupertino
First of six. Sells the low-yield, tax-heavy asset at a stepped-up basis (S18) with almost no capital-gains consequence ($0 of tax on a $6,531,230 sale, the residue being California's 0.11% county transfer tax), keeps the building that produces cash, and occupies the vacant unit so housing costs nothing beyond the rent forgone on that one unit.

What the grid shows

Plan F wins eight of nine combinations. It loses only at a 4% portfolio return — the case where keeping and renting Cupertino (B) edges ahead, because the house's own appreciation does more work than the investments. No combination makes selling both and renting (D) the best answer, and no combination makes renting both houses (C) competitive.

What the model cannot tell you

  • Whether you want to be a landlord. Plan F still leaves one building with two tenants. If the answer is no, plan D is the only plan that avoids it entirely, and it costs roughly $5,274,350 in terminal wealth.
  • Concentration. Plan F puts 38% of the estate into one three-unit building in one ZIP code. Diversification has a value the model does not price.
  • Liquidity and optionality. A portfolio can be partly sold; a house cannot.
  • Sentiment. The family home may be worth more to you than the numbers say. That is a legitimate input the model cannot accept.
  • Future tax law. Every rate in this model is today's rate. See the risk register →
