1. Property tax: Proposition 13

Proposition 13 does three things:

  • caps the general levy at 1% of full cash value. This one lives in article XIII A of the state constitution rather than in the statute, so the evidence here is the county rate books, which state “1% Maximum Tax Levy 1.00000” for every tax rate area (S09 S10);
  • limits annual increases in assessed value to 2% through an inflation factor at Revenue and Taxation Code §51(a)(1)(D), which provides that the increase shall in no event exceed 2% of the prior year's value (S17);
  • reassesses to market value only on a change in ownership or new construction.

Section 51 also makes the taxable value the lesser of the factored base year value and current full cash value. That is the mechanical basis for a Proposition 8 temporary decline, which this model never assumes and which is noted on the projections page.

The 1% is a cap, not a total. Voter-approved bond debt is added on top, and every parcel sits in a tax rate area that determines which bonds apply. That is why two houses of equal value in the same county can have different bills.

The formula

Where the fair market value at the date of transfer exceeds the parent's factored base year value plus the allowance, the excess is added to the base:

new taxable value = parent's factored base year value + max(0, market value − (base + allowance))

Which simplifies, when the cap is exceeded, to market value minus the allowance. That is why the allowance is the only number that matters for a high-value Bay Area house.

What disqualifies a claim

  • The house was not the transferor's principal residence.
  • You do not make it your principal residence within one year, or you do not claim the homeowners' exemption (S01).
  • The claim is filed after three years, or after the property is sold.
  • The property is a rental on the date of transfer. Prop 19 removed the exclusion for everything other than a principal residence and certain family farms.

3. Estate and inheritance tax

California: no estate tax, no inheritance tax

Proposition 6 (June 1982) eliminated California's inheritance and gift tax. The residual state "pick-up" tax produced no revenue for decedents dying after 2004, and the LAO states plainly that "California does not currently impose any estate taxes on estates of decedents dying after 2004" (S06). The Franchise Tax Board confirms that the Revenue and Taxation Code bars the state from imposing estate and gift taxes without a voter-approved constitutional amendment (S07).

The only death tax that can reach a California resident is the federal estate tax.

The federal rate above the exclusion is 40% (S03). The annual gift exclusion for 2026 is $19,000 per recipient; gifts within it never touch the lifetime exclusion.

What this means for an inheritance that includes "a considerable amount of money"

  • On current law and a 2026 death, nothing is due until the estate exceeds $15,000,000 for a single person or $30,000,000 for a married couple with portability.
  • The two houses are projected at $10,323,560 in 2045. Against an indexed single-person exclusion of $23,979,750, that leaves roughly $13,656,190 of cash before a return is required.
  • An inheritance is not income. Nothing is reported on your 1040 simply because you received cash or property. What is taxed later is the income those assets produce and the gain on assets sold after the step-up.
  • Two planning points that are worth more than the tax: elect portability on a timely Form 706 even when no tax is due, and do not gift the houses during life (see below).

4. Capital gains, step-up basis, and the home-sale exclusion

Property acquired from a decedent has a basis equal to its fair market value at the date of death (S18). Everything the parents' estate gained is erased for income-tax purposes.

If you later sell a house you have lived in

Up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 on a joint return) can be excluded if you owned and used the house as your main home for at least 24 of the last 60 months (S05). Because the basis has already stepped up, the gain to be sheltered is usually modest.

If you later sell a house you have rented

Depreciation taken while letting the property reduces the basis and is recaptured on sale. The model charges 25% federal on the recaptured amount plus California tax on it as ordinary income, and the blended rate on the rest.

The cliff at $5,000,000

A San Francisco sale at $4,990,000 is taxed at 0.75%, or about $37,430. A sale at $5,000,000 is taxed at 2.25%, or $112,500. One dollar of price costs $75,070 of tax. Projected values put the Outer Sunset building above $5,000,000 by about 2052 — which matters if you plan to sell it later rather than at inheritance.

6. Income tax on rent

Rental income is taxable. The model assumes a blended marginal rate of 45.1% on net positive rental income (federal 32% + 3.8% net investment income tax (S19) + California 9.3%), with no benefit taken for passive losses, which are generally suspended at higher income levels. Depreciation is modelled on a 27.5-year straight line applied to the building portion only, and California is assumed to conform.

What is verified here and what is not

The 3.8% net investment income tax and its thresholds are verified (S19), including the rule that a gain excluded under the home-sale provision is not net investment income. The 45.1% marginal stack, the 27.5-year period and the state-conformity assumption are modelling choices, not verified outcomes for your situation. They are stated here rather than buried because they affect the rental plans more than they affect plan F.
