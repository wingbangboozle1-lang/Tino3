Income tax on each path

Basis step-up, capital gains, the 121 exclusion, recapture.

The tax-free window

Property acquired from a decedent takes a basis equal to its value at death. Selling soon after the inheritance therefore produces almost no taxable gain: on the 2045 model the tax is $0 on a $6,531,230 sale. Hold the same house for thirty years and the tax becomes $4,910,950, even after the $250,000 exclusion. Let it out as well and depreciation recapture pushes it to $7,203,420.

That is the core of the recommendation. The window is not a loophole that might close; it is how the basis rules work, and it only exists at the moment of transfer.

Same house, same thirty years, three very different tax outcomes. The difference is entirely the basis reset at death plus the depreciation taken in the rental case.

What actually steps up: the assumption behind every sale figure

The model assumes the whole property gets a new basis at death. Three common facts would break that.

Every sale figure on this site — the near-zero tax on selling at the inheritance, the tax on holding for thirty years, the recapture on letting — assumes that the whole of Property A receives a basis equal to its value at the date of death. That is the ordinary rule, and it is probably right here. But it is an assumption about facts this site has never seen, and three patterns give only a partial step-up:

  • A co-owner who is not covered. If someone else owns a share of the house today — a joint tenant, a sibling, a surviving spouse whose share does not pass to the heir — only the share that passes from the decedent takes the new basis. The rest keeps its old, much lower basis, and a later sale carries gain on that part.
  • A life estate or a trust that does not give the heir the whole interest. A remainder that vests later, or an interest that is split, can leave part of the property outside the step-up entirely.
  • Community property where only part is in the gross estate. The double step-up is not automatic: it applies where at least one half of the whole community interest was includible in the decedent's gross estate. That is a fact about the estate, not about the deed.

None of these changes the property tax analysis, which depends on the change in ownership, not on the basis. All of them change the income tax on a later sale. The trust document and the deed decide which one applies.

The step-up is a federal income tax rule. It is separate from the property tax reassessment, which is a California rule about a change in ownership, and separate from the federal estate tax, which is a tax on the transfer. Keeping the three apart is most of the work on this page.

The alternate valuation date is a one-way door, and it is the executor's to walk

If values fall in the six months after the death, the executor can elect to value the estate then instead, but only if doing so reduces both the gross estate and the estate tax, and the election is irrevocable once made. It also sets the income tax basis, so it helps a later sale only if the estate itself was taxable. Where the estate is inside the exclusion — which is the case this site models for the house alone, though the owner has said the estate as a whole may not be — there is no estate tax to reduce, so the election is usually not available and not wanted. Take advice before the return is filed: the window closes when it is filed, and it cannot be reopened more than a year after the due date.

The letting path, taxed the way the rules actually say

What this site had been getting wrong about the letting path

Every other path on this site is priced after tax. The letting path was not. It credited the rent and deducted the property tax and the running costs, and then charged no income tax at all on what was left. At the same time it reduced the sale basis by thirty years of depreciation and charged recapture on that depreciation when the property was sold — a deduction it had never allowed in the first place. So the published letting path was generous in one direction, harsh in the other, and stated neither.

The rent input is still empty, so no published baseline figure was wrong: with no rent the path carries cost and no income, which is the conservative case. But the break-even rent the site published was computed on that untaxed basis, and the whole of the income tax on the rent was missing from it. This section prices the path the way the rules say and shows the difference.

What it changes

The published break-even rent was too low. Untaxed, the letting path needs $362,600 a year of rent to match selling at the inheritance and investing. Priced under the rules, it needs between $418,880 and $479,700 a year — up to $117,100 more, because the rent is taxed at ordinary rates rather than at the lower rates that apply to a gain. The lowest taxed figure is the most favourable case a letting landlord can hope for: every year's loss used immediately, at the lowest rate in the grid.

The best path does not move. In all 10 cases, selling at the inheritance and investing is still worth more than letting the house out by $23,545,750 or more at today's empty rent input, and by construction the two are equal at each case's own break-even rent. The tax rules change how far behind the letting path is, not that it is behind.

The passive loss rules bite below the break-even, not above it. At a rent of $321,130 a year or less the let throws off a tax loss every year, and that loss is suspended for California whatever the heir does, because California has no real estate professional exception. Above that rent the let is net positive and simply taxed. The letting path only reaches break-even at rents well above that line, so for anyone actually choosing between paths the loss rules are a cushion on the way down rather than a reason to let.

The one way the tax tail can be worse than the model shows. If the heir keeps the property until death instead of selling it, the suspended losses are allowed only to the extent they exceed the step-up in the transferee's basis — and a step-up to full market value leaves nothing. In that case the letting path is worth −$1,053,570 at today's empty rent input, exactly what the untaxed model says, with $10,725,000 of suspended losses simply lost.

The headline case is the one this site treats as most likely: the loss, where there is one, is suspended and allowed only on a complete sale, priced at the top combined ordinary rate. The first ten years are shown, then every fifth year, then the last. Note the shape: depreciation is a fixed nominal amount, so it shelters less of the rent each year, and when the building is fully recovered in year 28 the taxable result jumps.

What is assumed here, and what is not known

  • The household's marginal rate is not supplied. It is not assumed. Every case is run at three stated combined ordinary rates and the grid is the answer, not any one row of it.
  • Losses freed on a sale are assumed to be absorbed in that year. Where the released amount exceeds the year's income it becomes a net operating loss carryforward rather than a refund; this model treats it as absorbed, which is the favourable reading, and the reading is stated.
  • The rent input is empty. No market rent is invented anywhere on this page. The break-even figures are solved for; the path values are stated at the empty input.
  • Whether the heir would meet the 250-hour safe harbour, actively participate, or have modified adjusted gross income inside the $100,000 to $150,000 range is not known. All three are tested as separate cases rather than assumed either way.
  • The state and federal rules are simply added. No federal deduction is taken for the state tax, and no California adjustment is modelled for a deduction the state does not allow. Both choices are deliberately conservative.

The transfer tax and recording fees on the sell path

Priced, and smaller than the assumption it sits inside

The sell path carries a 5.5% selling cost. The brief asked for the transfer tax and the recording fees to be priced into that path explicitly rather than left inside the percentage, so they are itemised below. On a $5,000,000 sale they come to $5,520 in total, about 2.0% of the modelled selling cost. The other 98.0% is commission, escrow and title - quoted by private companies, not set by statute, and the part of the assumption that needs three real quotes before it is relied on.

One charge that checklists routinely add does not apply. The $75 fee on recorded real-estate instruments is expressly excluded where the instrument is recorded in connection with a transfer subject to the documentary transfer tax, which a sale is. It is listed as zero with the reason, because deleting it silently would hide the verification.

The 1% surcharge above $1,000,000, computed

The 1% is not a bracket, and it is already in every figure on this site

Revenue and Taxation Code section 17043 imposes an additional 1% on taxable income above $1,000,000. Two features of the statute matter here, and both are easy to get backwards:

  • It ignores filing status. Subsection (c) switches off the credit rules (section 17039), the filing-status and bracket-recomputation rules (section 17041) and the joint-return rules (section 17045). The threshold is therefore $1,000,000 for a single filer and $1,000,000 for a joint return, not $2,000,000.
  • It is not indexed. The statute sets a flat dollar amount with no inflation adjustment, while the brackets around it are indexed every year. Over a forty-year horizon the threshold reaches less and less income in today's money.

The published 2025 Form 540 rate schedules end at 1230.0% on all three filing-status schedules. Add the statute's 1% and the California layer is 1330.0% - which is exactly the state rate this model has been using, so the surcharge was never missing from the numbers. What was missing was the statement that it is there, and the check that its arithmetic was described the right way round. Both are corrected here.

The two rates are the ones in the inputs file: 3610.0% without the surcharge and 3710.0% with it. Both are applied to the whole gain, which is more conservative than a Form 540 computation, where the surcharge reaches only the part of taxable income above the threshold.

Does it change the recommendation?

No, and the table says why. On the path this site recommends - sell at inheritance - the basis step-up leaves a gain of about zero, so a tax on gain has nothing to tax and the surcharge's effect is $0. The surcharge bites on the two paths that hold the property for thirty years and then sell into a large gain. It makes those paths worse, so it pushes in the same direction as the recommendation rather than against it. The model's own check: selling at inheritance still beats living in it after thirty years is true.

A limit on this. This is a sensitivity on one assumption, computed the same conservative way as the rest of the model: the rate is applied to the entire gain. A real Form 540 would apply the top bracket and the surcharge only to the slices of income that fall in them, and would deduct the gain basis and allowable expenses first. A CPA should compute the actual year-of-sale return; the direction of the result is robust, the magnitude is an upper bound.

What the model does and does not do

Not modelled, and flagged for a professional

  • A like-kind exchange under section 1031. Exchanges of real property are still available, but they defer rather than forgive tax, and they do not fix the property tax reassessment. Whether one makes sense depends on whether the heir wants to stay in real estate at all.
  • An instalment sale. Spreads the gain, but not the tax.
  • Gifting during life rather than at death. Removes the basis step-up. Almost always worse for an appreciated asset.
  • Whether the trust itself should hold the property after the transfer. This affects both the basis and the Proposition 19 claim and depends on the trust terms, which this site does not have.
  • The federal deduction for state tax. Not taken. Taking it would slightly reduce the combined rate.
