Income tax on each path

Basis step-up, capital gains, the 121 exclusion, recapture.

The tax-free window

Property acquired from a decedent takes a basis equal to its value at death. Selling soon after the inheritance therefore produces almost no taxable gain: on the 2045 model the tax is $0 on a $6,531,230 sale. Hold the same house for thirty years and the tax becomes $4,910,950, even after the $250,000 exclusion. Let it out as well and depreciation recapture pushes it to $7,203,420.

That is the core of the recommendation. The window is not a loophole that might close; it is how the basis rules work, and it only exists at the moment of transfer.

The transfer tax and recording fees on the sell path

Priced, and smaller than the assumption it sits inside

The sell path carries a 5.5% selling cost. The brief asked for the transfer tax and the recording fees to be priced into that path explicitly rather than left inside the percentage, so they are itemised below. On a $5,000,000 sale they come to $5,520 in total, about 2.0% of the modelled selling cost. The other 98.0% is commission, escrow and title - quoted by private companies, not set by statute, and the part of the assumption that needs three real quotes before it is relied on.

One charge that checklists routinely add does not apply. The $75 fee on recorded real-estate instruments is expressly excluded where the instrument is recorded in connection with a transfer subject to the documentary transfer tax, which a sale is. It is listed as zero with the reason, because deleting it silently would hide the verification.

The 1% surcharge above $1,000,000, computed

The 1% is not a bracket, and it is already in every figure on this site

Revenue and Taxation Code section 17043 imposes an additional 1% on taxable income above $1,000,000. Two features of the statute matter here, and both are easy to get backwards:

  • It ignores filing status. Subsection (c) switches off the credit rules (section 17039), the filing-status and bracket-recomputation rules (section 17041) and the joint-return rules (section 17045). The threshold is therefore $1,000,000 for a single filer and $1,000,000 for a joint return, not $2,000,000.
  • It is not indexed. The statute sets a flat dollar amount with no inflation adjustment, while the brackets around it are indexed every year. Over a forty-year horizon the threshold reaches less and less income in today's money.

The published 2025 Form 540 rate schedules end at 1230.0% on all three filing-status schedules. Add the statute's 1% and the California layer is 1330.0% - which is exactly the state rate this model has been using, so the surcharge was never missing from the numbers. What was missing was the statement that it is there, and the check that its arithmetic was described the right way round. Both are corrected here.

The two rates are the ones in the inputs file: 3610.0% without the surcharge and 3710.0% with it. Both are applied to the whole gain, which is more conservative than a Form 540 computation, where the surcharge reaches only the part of taxable income above the threshold.

Does it change the recommendation?

No, and the table says why. On the path this site recommends - sell at inheritance - the basis step-up leaves a gain of about zero, so a tax on gain has nothing to tax and the surcharge's effect is $0. The surcharge bites on the two paths that hold the property for thirty years and then sell into a large gain. It makes those paths worse, so it pushes in the same direction as the recommendation rather than against it. The model's own check: selling at inheritance still beats living in it after thirty years is true.

A limit on this. This is a sensitivity on one assumption, computed the same conservative way as the rest of the model: the rate is applied to the entire gain. A real Form 540 would apply the top bracket and the surcharge only to the slices of income that fall in them, and would deduct the gain basis and allowable expenses first. A CPA should compute the actual year-of-sale return; the direction of the result is robust, the magnitude is an upper bound.

What the model does and does not do

Not modelled, and flagged for a professional

  • A like-kind exchange under section 1031. Exchanges of real property are still available, but they defer rather than forgive tax, and they do not fix the property tax reassessment. Whether one makes sense depends on whether the heir wants to stay in real estate at all.
  • An instalment sale. Spreads the gain, but not the tax.
  • Gifting during life rather than at death. Removes the basis step-up. Almost always worse for an appreciated asset.
  • Whether the trust itself should hold the property after the transfer. This affects both the basis and the Proposition 19 claim and depends on the trust terms, which this site does not have.
  • The federal deduction for state tax. Not taken. Taking it would slightly reduce the combined rate.
