Estate liquidity and borrowing

What the estate tax costs, when it is due, and how it can be paid without a forced sale.

The nine-month clock

Federal estate tax is 40% of the amount above the exclusion, and it is due 9 months after the date of death. A six-month extension of time to file the return is available on request, but it is not an extension of time to pay: interest runs on anything unpaid from the original due date, whether or not an extension was granted.

The interest rate is not a penalty rate and not a fixed one. It is the federal short-term rate plus three percentage points, set every quarter, and it compounds daily. The rate published for the fourth quarter of 2026 is 7%, on a federal short-term rate of 4%. The published range across 2022 to 2026 runs from 3% to 8%.

The keep-and-borrow case, run end to end

The interest rows above price the carrying cost of borrowed money. This case answers the question that table cannot: if the estate owes one of the published bands, is it cheaper to keep the house and borrow the band's tax against it, or to sell the house and pay the tax out of the proceeds? Both routes are run end to end on the same engine that prices every path on the decision page, over the same thirty-year horizon, at the 2045 model.

The findings, in order:

  • Borrowing never becomes the better route at any published band. At the modelled return the payment stream compounds to about 6.7 times the principal in future value: even the smallest band's loan costs $2,691,700 of terminal value against a $33,166,570 selling-versus-living gap. The mortgage decision is therefore about liquidity, risk and the household's own circumstances - not about which route leaves more.
  • The largest published band is the one the sale route cannot self-fund. The projected net proceeds at the 2045 model are $6,172,010, and that band's tax is $8,000,000, so the shortfall would have to come from other assets or from a loan secured by both buildings - about 77.5% of their combined projected value. That conversation has to happen before the nine-month date arrives, not during it.
  • The loan-to-value arithmetic is published, not assumed away. The two largest bands exceed the projected value of Property A alone, so borrowing against that one property cannot raise them.

The last column is the future value of the same payment stream at the model's after-tax equity return: what the money would have been worth if it had never left the portfolio. The loan-to-value columns compare each band's loan with the properties' projected values at the 2045 model ($6,531,230 for Property A alone and $10,323,560 for both together); they are arithmetic, not a statement that any lender would lend. A rate for the 2040s is not published and is not forecast: the loan is priced at the rate published today, held flat for thirty years, which no real fixed-rate borrower in 2045 would experience. Whether a lender would lend the larger bands against either building, and on what terms, is not modelled and is not assumed: the largest bands exceed the projected value of Property A alone, so they would need both buildings as collateral or other assets besides. What this page cannot tell you

  • Whether the estate owes anything at all. The estate's size is not published and not modelled. Read the bands and apply the estate's own number privately.
  • Whether prior taxable gifts have already used part of the exclusion. Lifetime taxable gifts are added back in the computation, so the published exclusion is the most the estate can shelter, not necessarily what it will shelter. Question F on the questions page.
  • Whether the section 6166 installment election is available. It requires an interest in a closely held business exceeding 35% of the adjusted gross estate. A home occupied as a personal residence is not that on the face of the statute, so this site does not assume the election - but if any part of the estate is an operating business, ask the attorney before assuming it is not.
  • Whether a lender will lend against a property held in trust. That is a question for the lender and the trustee, and the answer decides whether the borrowing rows above are available at all. The loan-to-value columns in the keep-and-borrow tables are arithmetic, not underwriting: the two largest published bands exceed Property A's projected value alone, so they would need both buildings as collateral or other assets besides.
  • What borrowing costs when it is not forced by a tax bill. The rows above price borrowing to meet the nine-month clock, band by band. Borrowing to take the cash out anyway, while keeping the home - the discretionary case, where the money is invested rather than paid to a tax authority - is a different question with a different answer, and it is run end to end on the keep-and-borrow page: loan-to-value rungs, amortisation terms, an interest-only line and the hurdle return the borrowed money has to beat.
  • What any of these rates will be in the 2040s. Every rate on this page is the published rate today, or a published range. None of them is a forecast.
  • Whether interest on borrowed money is deductible. This site assumes no deduction, which is the conservative reading. The actual answer depends on what the borrowing is secured by and what it is used for.
  • What is in the estate. The blank inventory at the foot of this page is deliberately empty. Filling it in is the household's work, and it stays private.

How the numbers on this page are produced

Every computed figure on this page comes from scripts/model.py, which reads assets/data/inputs.json and assets/data/series.json. Monetary results are rounded to the nearest $10 before they are printed. The rules the model implements are the published official rules below; where a page uses a stated assumption or an unverified owner figure, that row says so and is logged on the register of unverified and assumed items.

  • California Legislative Information - Revenue and Taxation Code section 63.2
  • California State Board of Equalization - Publication 29, Property Taxpayers' Guide
  • Internal Revenue Service - Gifts and inheritances - inherited property basis and sale
  • Internal Revenue Service - Topic no. 409, Capital gains and losses
