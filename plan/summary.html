Recommended plan

  1. Now, while the owner is alive: change nothing about title. Property passing at death gets a new income-tax basis equal to its value at the date of death, so the gain built up over the owner's lifetime is never taxed. Property A alone is $11,900,000 under the 2026 federal exclusion; California has no estate or inheritance tax. Property B is not part of this: the owner states it already belongs to the heir.
  2. Keep Property B. It is already the heir's, so the inheritance does not reassess it and does not reset its income-tax basis. Net rent is 4.27% of its value today on the old assessed value; with the lowest house-price growth tested that is 6.37% a year, above the 4.98% 10-year Treasury yield, and the third unit is the heir's home. A sale of B would be taxed on the heir's own gain, which a sale of A after inheritance is not.
  3. Property A, default: sell within the first year after inheritance and reinvest. In that window the sale is almost free of capital-gains tax, and it removes a property-tax bill that jumps from $10,700 to between $48,600 and $74,110 a year (2045, base case). Before income tax, selling beats living in the house by $1,004,330 over ten years.
  4. Switch to “keep and rent out” only if a written rent estimate at that time is above about 2.52% of the house's value per year ($9,020 a month in today's dollars for 2045). No verified market rent exists today, so this cell is deliberately blank.
  5. Switch to “live in it” only if you expect the house to appreciate faster than about 5.01% a year, or your tax rate on interest income is above about 27% and the sale money would sit in taxable bonds. Then move in and file the Proposition 19 claim and homeowners' exemption within one year.
  6. If still undecided nine months after the death, move in and file. The exclusion cannot be claimed later, while the option to sell stays open.
  7. Decide with the conditions of that year, not with an average. Replayed along all 41 ten-year stretches of the official record since 1975, selling came out ahead in 23 and keeping in 18. Selling won every time house prices had risen 5% a year or more over the five years before; keeping won every time they had fallen. See the uneven-economy test.

The call is close: selling wins 9 of the 18 steady-growth cases tested (three inheritance years, three appreciation rates, before and after tax). The numbers are on the decision page; everything that could not be verified is on the Flagged for Review page.
