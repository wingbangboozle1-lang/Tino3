The answer, first

Sell Property A soon after it is inherited. Living in it, letting it out, and every mixed version of those two end behind selling in the baseline and in 93% of 2000 resampled histories of the verified record, on the distribution section. The one exception the household controls is whether it wants to live in this particular house.

Confidence: high · Computed 15 November 2026 from the inputs on this site · It moves only if one of these changes:

  • The transfer is not parent-to-child. Then there is no Proposition 19 exclusion, the property is reassessed in full, and selling wins by more. Question A-1.
  • Market rent clears $30,220 a month in 2045 dollars. Then letting it out matches selling. The pre-commitment rule P-5.
  • House prices grow faster than 8.66% a year for thirty years. Then keeping it matches selling at a 7% return. Break-even.
  • The household decides it wants to live there. That is a legitimate reason, and it is the one input no model can price. Rule P-3.

The one-line answer

Under every baseline this site can build, selling Property A soon after it is inherited beats living in it or letting it out, and it is not close. The reason is a one-off combination: the inheritance resets the income-tax basis to the value at death, so an immediate sale pays almost no capital gains tax, while the property tax assessment jumps to market value under Proposition 19. Waiting converts a tax-free asset into a taxed one and adds roughly $68,490 a year of extra property tax if the home is let out.

The break-even house growth needed to make keeping the home beat selling is 8.66% a year for thirty years. The verified California state house price index compounded at 6.4% a year over the fifty years to 2025 and 2.6% a year over the last twenty. Read the full decision, then the cases where this flips.

Reinvestment risk beside the headline. Selling and investing assumes the proceeds can be reinvested at the modelled equity return. Inheriting into a 1929- or 2008-style crash means buying equities at the top. The phased path on the black-swan page parks the proceeds in short-term Treasuries for 24-36 months, then moves them into stocks, which softens crash-entry risk - at the cost of missing out while equities rally. On the 2045 baseline the proceeds could lose 0.49% a year and selling would still match living, so the case for selling does not need a high return. Break-even case.

The three checks that come before any number

One fact is assumed, not verified, and everything else depends on it

The Proposition 19 exclusion applies only where the transfer is between a parent and a child, or between a grandparent and a grandchild whose parent has died. If that is not the relationship here, there is no exclusion: the property is reassessed to full market value, every shelter figure on this site disappears, and the case for selling gets stronger. The trust document is the only thing that settles it. Answer question A-1 before relying on any number here. California Legislative Information

What would change the answer

  1. The Proposition 19 exclusion does not apply. Then the property is reassessed in full, every shelter figure on this site falls away, and selling gets stronger. Question A-1 settles it.
  2. House prices in this area grow faster than 8.66% a year for thirty years. Then keeping the home matches selling at a 7% return. Break-even case.
  3. Market rent reaches $30,220 a month in 2045 dollars. Then letting the home out matches selling. Break-even case.
  4. A regime like the one in the stress cases recurs. Keeping the home wins in 2 of 6 stress regimes: Great Inflation repeat; A fast inflation burst then a rate shock. Black swan conditions.
  5. The crash arrives before the sale proceeds are invested. Replaying the real 1929, 2000 and 2008 records, parking the proceeds for 24-36 months and phasing into equities beat investing on day one; in the 2020 record it lost. Real entry-timing replays.
  6. The heir genuinely wants to live in the house. The one-line recommendation is conditional on that. The imputed-rent break-even prices what that preference has to be worth.
  7. Moving out after moving in. The Proposition 19 exclusion is removed when the heir stops qualifying, and the model now costs that at about $244,980 of extra property tax over thirty years. Mixed paths.
  8. The housing the house provides is worth close to its full market rent to the heir. The published comparison credits the living path with no housing at all. Credited at about 100.00% of market rent, living and selling come level; below that, selling wins. The rent living does not pay itself.
  9. Property B's fixed charges keep growing at their own pace. The model assumes 2.5% a year; the audited record is 8.1% over nine years, and those charges are 44.7% of that bill. The 2% cap cannot bound them. Roll audit.

How to read this site

  1. Start with the executive summary and the decision page.
  2. Check the inputs. Every number the model uses is on the Property A, Property B and costs pages, with its source or an explicit unverified flag.
  3. Check the stress. No projection on this site relies on a single smooth growth rate. See black swan conditions and the verified historical record.
  4. Do not act yet. The questions page lists what is still unknown and how to find it out. The suggestions page is a review queue: nothing there has been implemented.

How the numbers on this page are produced

Every computed figure on this page comes from scripts/model.py, which reads assets/data/inputs.json and assets/data/series.json. Monetary results are rounded to the nearest $10 before they are printed. The rules the model implements are the published official rules below; where a page uses a stated assumption or an unverified owner figure, that row says so and is logged on the register of unverified and assumed items.
