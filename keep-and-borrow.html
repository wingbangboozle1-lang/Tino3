Keep the house, take the cash

Take the cash out of the house without selling it, and what the loan costs for the privilege.

What borrowing instead of selling is worth

The path exists, it is priced, and on this model it does not win. A cash-out refinance at 60% of the home's value at a 2045 transfer releases $3,918,740 of cash while the heir keeps living in the house, keeps the sheltered assessed value and pays no income tax on the release. What it costs is the spread between the loan rate and what the borrowed money earns: at the published mortgage average of 7.11% and the model's after-tax portfolio return of 6.30% a year, the overlay is −$1,287,750 over 30 years - a loss, not a gain.

What would change that answer is one number: the hurdle. Borrowing pays for itself only if the borrowed money earns more than 6.80% a year after tax. At the lowest mortgage average published in 2026 (5.98%) the same rung turns positive by $1,442,820; at the highest (7.40%) it loses $2,004,440. So the question this page answers is not "should the heir borrow" but "can the household put the money to work at better than the loan charges it". Almost certainly not, after tax, which is why every rung here sits behind the selling path - between $31,242,810 and $36,215,460 behind at a 2045 transfer.

The reason to read this page anyway is the reason nobody models: a household that needs cash and does not want to sell into whatever market it inherits into. Borrowing releases $3,559,520 more cash than a sale would on the same day, because a sale pays the $359,220 selling cost and a loan pays none of it - and the tax on the release is nil on both paths, because the basis was already stepped up to the value at the date of death.

Do not read this page as the other borrowing case, which is also published. The estate liquidity page runs what happens when a band of tax has to be paid within nine months and the household borrows against the kept house instead of selling to raise the money - a fully amortising 30-year loan at the published average, set against selling and paying that same band out of the proceeds, and it is dearer at every published band. That is forced borrowing, and its comparator is a sale under a deadline. This page is discretionary borrowing: no bill is due, the household wants the cash and the house, and the money is invested. The two cases share the loan arithmetic and reach the same conclusion, but they answer different questions and neither is the other's evidence.

How this page is computed, so it can be checked

This is not a second model. The terminal value of the living path, its property-tax stream, the stepped-up basis and the selling cost are all read straight out of assets/data/model.json, the same file the dashboard, the decision page and the mixed-path page use. On top of that terminal this engine adds one overlay:

overlay = loan × (1 + r)30 − the future value at r of every annual payment − the balance still owed at year 30

r is the after-tax portfolio return the whole site uses (6.30%: the gross equity assumption less the tax drag). The payment is the amortising payment at the published mortgage average, computed monthly from a rate compounded monthly and paid annually. A loan whose term equals the horizon is repaid exactly, so its balance term is nil, and the hurdle it implies sits at its own rate - which is the arithmetic check that the engine is right: at 7.11% the hurdle is 6.80%, the difference being the timing of annual payments against monthly compounding.

Nothing on this page is an offer. Loan-to-value rungs of 40, 60 and 80 percent are stated scenarios; the rates are the averages the publishers themselves state, not a quote for this household. The loan in the model's own forced-borrowing case, on the estate liquidity page and as lever 6 of the what-if page, is sized to a tax band rather than to a share of the value, which is why the two sets of figures do not line up row for row; both are charged against the same living-path terminal from model.json, and both treat the interest as non-deductible.

Read the last two columns together with the two overlay columns. An overlay is what the loan is worth on top of the living path, positive or negative. Parked in the short Treasuries this site uses for the phased path, every rung loses money, because the parking yield is below the loan rate. Spent rather than invested, the loss is the cost of the money column, which is the honest figure for a household that borrows to consume. The hurdle is the after-tax portfolio return at which the overlay is exactly zero.

A fifteen-year amortisation costs $2,377,390 of interest against $5,361,410 on a thirty-year one, and needs $419,740 a year instead of $309,340. The same arithmetic on the building the heir already owns

Nothing about the overlay is specific to the inherited house. A $1,080,000 cash-out at 7.11% against a $1,800,000 building costs $85,250 a year in payments, which is 0.99× the $85,800 of annual rent the building currently collects. That is the comparison worth making before any borrowing is discussed: the rent covers the payment almost exactly, but the payment also repays principal while the rent does not, and a rental building's cash-out is a different risk profile from a home the heir lives in. Property B's own leases, units and local rules are unresolved questions, not inputs - see the Property B page.

What this page cannot tell you

  • Whether any lender will do this. The rungs are stated loan-to-value scenarios, not quotations. Loan-to-value rungs of 40, 60 and 80 percent are stated scenarios. No official source publishes what a lender would advance against this property, and this site does not assume a quote exists. Debt-to-income limits, mortgage insurance, prepayment penalties, closing costs and whether a trust-held property is eligible at all are not modelled. Closing costs on a refinance are the one number a lender must supply before any figure here means anything.
  • Whether the proceeds are actually invested. The overlay funds the payment from the borrowed money itself. If the household spends the proceeds, the payment has to come from income, and the cost of the money column is the honest figure to read instead.
  • What the interest costs after tax. Publication 936 allows a deduction only where the money was used to buy, build or substantially improve the home securing the loan, and caps home acquisition debt at $750,000 for mortgages taken out after 15 December 2017. Cash taken out to invest is outside that. Whether any part of it is deductible as investment interest is a question for the preparer, and this page assumes none.
  • Whether the property can be pledged at all while it sits in a trust. That is a question for the trustee and the lender, and it is the same question the estate liquidity page raises for paying estate tax by borrowing.
  • Any of the future rates. Every rate on this page is a published average for 2026, read on 17 November 2026, not a forecast for the 2040s. The ranking of the paths does not turn on the rate: it turns on the spread between the rate and the return, and on the fact that a kept house still carries its own bill.

How the numbers on this page are produced

Every computed figure on this page comes from scripts/model.py, which reads assets/data/inputs.json and assets/data/series.json. Monetary results are rounded to the nearest $10 before they are printed. The rules the model implements are the published official rules below; where a page uses a stated assumption or an unverified owner figure, that row says so and is logged on the register of unverified and assumed items.
