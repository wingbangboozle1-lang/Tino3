The decision

Five paths, priced, with the break-even numbers and the sensitivity.

First gate: is the transfer between a parent and a child?

Every reassessment figure on this site assumes the transfer will qualify for the intergenerational exclusion in Revenue and Taxation Code section 63.2. That section excludes a transfer only where it is between parents and their children, or between grandparents and grandchildren where every parent of the grandchild who is a child of the grandparent has died. “Children” is defined in the statute to include a stepchild, a son-in-law or daughter-in-law, and an adopted child adopted before age 18; it also includes a foster child of a licensed foster parent where adoption was barred. The relationship is a condition of the exclusion, not a detail: if the transferor and the heir are not in one of those relationships, no exclusion is available, the property is reassessed to full market value, and the entire saving shown on this page disappears.

This analysis is written on the assumption that it is. The trust document is the only thing that confirms it, and this site has not seen it. Until it is confirmed, treat every Proposition 19 shelter figure here as conditional. See question A-1.

The living path, and the rent it does not pay itself

The published comparison charges the living path for the house and credits it with nothing

If the heir lives in the house, the heir is also consuming housing. A comparison that charges every cost of owning and credits none of that consumption makes living look worse than it is by exactly the value of the roof. This site publishes that version - zero imputed rent - because it is the conservative one, and it says so rather than letting the omission hide inside a table.

What this changes, and what it does not

It does not change the published recommendation, but it narrows it in a way worth stating. Selling still wins unless the housing is credited at about 100% of market rent - that is, unless the heir values living in this particular house at essentially the full market rent it would fetch. At that point the two paths are level, and the decision stops being financial. A household that would genuinely pay $30,140 a month to live there has a different answer from one that would not, and neither answer is wrong.

What it does not fix. The credit is still an assumption about what the household would otherwise pay, and there is still no market rent for the home in the model. Filling both from one written rental opinion is the way to close it, and it is the first open question on the questions page.

What this site recommends

Sell soon after the inheritance. It leads in the baseline, in 9 of the 9 sensitivity combinations, and in 4 of the 6 stress regimes. It is also the only path that does not depend on the heir wanting to live in this particular house, being able to keep it as a principal residence, or finding a tenant at an implausible rent.

Terminal value is what is left at the end of the thirty years. On the sell paths it is the sale proceeds after selling costs, grown at the return shown. On the living and letting paths it is the sale after selling costs and capital gains tax, less the compounded future value of every year's property tax and running costs (and, for letting, plus the net rent). The column "Carrying tax and running costs" is their nominal total; on the sell paths it is the cost of the sale at inheritance, already netted from the proceeds. Nominal figures in the money of that future year.

The letting paths are credited with no rent at all

No market rent for Property A has been supplied, and no official source publishes a market rent for a specific home, so this site does not invent one. The consequence is that both letting paths carry every landlord cost and zero rental income. They are therefore not a test of whether letting the house works; they are a floor.

The break-even rent is the honest figure: about $30,140 a month in 2045, roughly $361,720 a year, to end thirty years in the same position as selling and investing. That is about 5.5% of the property's value in gross rent, every year. Whether the market clears that is the open question, and it is the one question that could move the recommendation. See suggestion S-06; the input that would be used is named and empty in the data file, so a documented rent opinion can be added and the whole model rebuilt.

Until then, read every statement that letting is the weakest path as the weakest path at zero rent.

Mixed paths: what households actually do

Why pure paths are not enough

The five paths above assume the household commits to one thing for thirty years. That is a useful way to price the extremes, and it is not what anybody does. A household may live in the house for five years and then sell, or live in it and later let it out. Those paths are priced here on the same engine and the same conventions, so “how long would we have to live there before it pays?” is answerable rather than rhetorical.

The two things the pure paths leave out

Living in it has value. The terminal-value columns on this page do not credit the years the heir spends housed. The imputed-rent table on the pre-commitment page prices that benefit across a range of market rents, and the rent needed to close the gap is in the break-even table above.

The property-tax relief can be taken back. Moving out is not free: section 63.2(a)(1)(C) enrols the change-of-ownership base year value once the heir stops qualifying. The clawback table prices it. It does not change the recommendation, but it moves the live-then-let path by six figures and it would be a mistake to model that path without it.

Keeping the house and borrowing against it

Why this path is here

The brief asks the comparison to carry debt, and the register said that this site priced the carrying cost of borrowing without ever running the path. It is run here end to end, because for a household that already owns Property B the interesting question is not only "sell or keep" but "keep, and take the money out of the wall instead of out of the sale". Borrowing is genuinely attractive on one axis that this model rewards: nothing is sold, so no selling cost is paid, no capital gains tax is triggered, and Revenue and Taxation Code section 62 says the security interest is not itself a change in ownership, so the property tax is the living path's tax and nothing more.

The home is kept exactly as in the living path - same Proposition 19 shelter, same projected tax bill, same running costs - and 7.11% fixed money is borrowed against it on a 30-year level-payment schedule, with 1.0% of the loan taken as origination and closing charges. The cash left after those charges is invested at the model's after-tax equity return, 6.3% a year, and the payments are deducted as a compounded outflow at the same rate, which is the convention every other path on this site uses. Terminal value is the living path's terminal, plus what the cash becomes, less what the payments cost, less any balance still owed. The loan-to-value rungs are stated scenarios, not lender policy and not an official maximum.

The verdict on the leveraged keep

It does not beat selling, at any return this model can reach, and at the published rate it does not even beat simply living there. At the middle rung the leveraged keep ends $1,763,620 below the unleveraged one. The reason is arithmetic, not pessimism: the loan costs 7.11% and the same money invested in the model's equity case returns 6.3% a year after its tax drag - a negative carry of 0.81 percentage points, before the 1.0% of charges. The break-even is 7.12% a year after tax on the borrowed money - the return at which borrowing to invest stops being a drag on the keep. The model's own equity case does not reach it.

Against selling, there is no break-even at all. The gap is $2,175,110 at minus 2% a year on the proceeds and $9,996,243,840 at plus 30% a year: the band was tested across that whole range and never crosses, because a lender advances a share of the value while a sale realises the whole of it less costs, and both sums then compound at the same rate while only one of them carries a payment. The leveraged keep is a worse way to hold the same house, and it is a worse way to raise the same cash.

How to read the balance and the payment columns

What this section does not say

It does not say the household could get such a loan in 2045, and it does not say the borrowed money would be invested as calmly as the arithmetic assumes. Both are the real constraints: a lender applies an income test and an appraisal to a cash-out refinance on an inherited house, and a borrower with a payment and a falling market behaves differently from a spreadsheet. The path is priced to close a gap in the analysis, and the finding - that the leveraged keep is dominated at every rung tested - is reported with those limits, not around them.

Reinvestment risk, beside the staging number

The other side of the staged-entry trade

Every staged figure on this site is a risk result, not a free improvement. On the 2045 baseline the sale proceeds are $38,585,540 if invested at once and $34,639,310 if parked in short-term Treasuries for 30 months and then phased in over 12 - a cost of $3,946,230 paid as insurance against inheriting into a 1929- or 2008-style peak. When the crash does come, the same staging is worth more than it cost; the dated replays on the black swan page show it winning in three of four episodes and losing in the 2020 one.

Two assumptions carry that result and neither is guaranteed. The parking yield - currently 4.2% a year, band 3.5% to 5.0% - is a stated scenario, not a published forward rate, and short Treasury yields in 2045 may be far lower. And the proceeds have to be reinvested on a written policy, which is a decision the household makes at the worst possible moment unless it writes one in advance (rule P-2).

Debt, occupancy, capital costs and liquidity

Why this section exists

The comparison the owner asked for is to carry taxes, debt, occupancy changes, capital costs and liquidity. Taxes, occupancy, capital costs and liquidity are all priced somewhere in this edition; debt was not - it simply was not mentioned, which is the worst way for a model to make an assumption, because a reader cannot see it. This section states the assumption in the open: both buildings are modelled free and clear, because no encumbrance was reported either way. It also prices what an encumbrance would do, per $1,000 a month of payment, so the household can read its own balance off the table without the model guessing at it: each $1,000 a month of payment carried while the house is kept is $1,311,830 of terminal value, and nothing at all on the sale path.

The direction of the effect is worth stating plainly, because it is counter-intuitive and it favours the recommendation. Debt reduces what a sale puts in the bank, but it also reduces what keeping the building is worth, by more: the holder pays the interest as well as the principal, and pays it out of money that would otherwise have been invested. So an encumbrance never makes keeping the house the better choice; it can only make it worse. That is why the ranking below does not change at any payment level.

What the model leaves out

  • Housing value. Living in the house produces somewhere to live. The break-even table prices that value; the terminal-value table does not add it in.
  • Taxes on the invested money. The equity path already carries an annual drag. A further tax on final liquidation is not charged, because the same treatment would have to be applied to the property paths and would be avoided anyway if the portfolio is held until death.
  • State and federal interaction. The combined gain tax rate simply adds the two rates and takes no federal deduction for the state tax. That is deliberately conservative.
  • Mortgages and other encumbrances, 1031 exchanges, instalment sales, gifting and trust restructuring. None is assumed away silently: the debt assumption is stated and priced per $1,000 a month of payment in Debt, occupancy, capital costs and liquidity; the path in which the heir keeps the home and takes cash out against its equity instead of selling is run end to end in Keeping the house and borrowing against it; and the rest are on the questions page and the suggestion queue.
  • Transaction costs of moving house, and the cost of capital gains tax on any other asset that would have to be sold. Not modelled.
