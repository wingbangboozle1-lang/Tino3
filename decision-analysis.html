Decision analysis

Three paths, one method: hold everything else constant, tax everything that is taxed, and compare what you would actually own twenty years after the death.

What is compared, and what is not

Compared: Property A only. Every path starts the day you inherit it and ends twenty years later, after selling costs, capital-gains tax, property tax, insurance, water, electricity and repairs — all of which are separately projected on the other pages.

Not compared: Property B's value, which is identical in every path. The one difference is its owner-occupied unit: if you move to Cupertino that unit can be let, so the live-in path is credited with $3,580 a month of rent (derived from the owner's own rent roll, F-07).

Deliberately excluded: mortgage financing (none assumed), passive-activity loss limits, the alternative minimum tax, and any income tax on retirement accounts. They are listed as flags rather than assumed away.

The decision rule

Sell. The measured outcome is better than every alternative in the base case, and selling is the only path whose return does not depend on a rent forecast or a 20-year appreciation assumption — it converts the house into a verified 4.88% yield on the day you inherit it.

Rent instead only if two things are true: two written rental appraisals clear $31,650 a month in 2045 dollars, and you expect Cupertino values to compound at 5.4% or better. At the 2026 one-year reading of +0.9%, that is a big assumption.

Live in it only as a lifestyle decision. It is the weakest financial path by about $251,180 a year at the base case. If living in that house is worth that to you, take it — but take it knowing the price.
