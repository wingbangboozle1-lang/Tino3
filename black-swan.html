Black swan conditions

Regimes where the answer changes, and by how much.

Why a smooth 4% line is the wrong tool

Real economies do not grow at a constant rate. In the verified record used here, annual consumer price inflation ranged from minus 0.3% to plus 13.5%, and annual house price growth in the California state record ranged from minus 20.0% to plus 25.3%. A single constant rate hides the thing that actually decides this case, which is whether the house and the stock market take their bad years at the same time.

Entry timing in the real record: 1929, 2000, 2008 and 2020

Real crashes, real prices, no invented percentages

The earlier version of this test used an invented crash: -40% then 25% then 10%. It is kept on this page as a stress case, but it was never evidence. This section replaces it with the real thing: the published monthly price of an index through the actual 1929, 2000, 2008 and 2020 episodes, and the published short-term cash rate for every month the money is waiting.

The question is the owner's question. If the sale proceeds arrive just before a crash, does parking them in short-term Treasuries for 24-36 months and then phasing into equities beat investing everything on day one? On the recorded crashes the answer is yes in 3 of 4 episodes for the phased path and yes in 3 of 4 for at least one of the two waiting strategies. But it is not free and it is not automatic, and the tables below show exactly where it stops working.

What the real replays say

  • The 1929 case is not close. The Dow Jones average was worth $1,035,490 in today's money to a buyer who invested everything at the September 1929 peak, and $6,918,560 for the same money parked for thirty months and then phased in. The parked money bought the index after the fall; the day-one money bought it before.
  • Waiting works when the crash comes early and the recovery is slow. That is 1929, 2000 and, more mildly, 2008.
  • Waiting loses when the crash is over before the parking period ends. In the 2020 episode the market had recovered before a 24-month park was complete, and phasing in cost $579,090 of today's purchasing power against investing on day one.
  • Parking longer is not better. The 34-month parked column beats the 30-month phased column in three of the four episodes, because it buys at the bottom with the whole balance rather than dribbling in as prices recover. That is a result of these four crashes, not a rule: it depends entirely on when the trough falls.

The honest limit: nobody inherits at the peak

The rows above assume the money arrives in the month before the crash. That is the best possible case for parking, and it is not a forecast. The table below moves the entry forward by one and two years and re-runs the same comparison. Once the entry lands after the fall, waiting has nothing to protect the money from, and it starts to cost: in the 2008 episode an heir who inherited twelve months later, in October 2008, would have been $3,362,610 better off investing everything immediately.

So the phased path is best understood as insurance against one specific bad luck - inheriting into the first year of a long bear market - paid for in the years when that luck does not happen. The price of that insurance is visible in the 2020 rows and in the later-entry rows.

How these replays were computed, and what they leave out

  1. Verbatim data. Each series was downloaded from the publisher's own CSV endpoint on 15 November 2026 and stored unchanged in assets/data/episodes/. If a month the replay needs is missing, the module stops with an error rather than interpolating. The provenance note beside the data lists every series.
  2. The three strategies. Day one: the whole amount buys the index at the entry month's close. Phased: the amount earns the published short cash rate for 30 months, then buys the index in 12 equal monthly purchases. Parked: the same, but the whole balance buys in after 34 months. Nothing is sold at any point, so all three end holding the same index at the exit month and differ only in how many units the money bought.
  3. Purchasing power, not nominal dollars. Each column is restated in October 2026 purchasing power using the published consumer price index. Comparing nominal dollars across a 1929-1959 window would make the 1929 result look larger for no reason other than inflation.
  4. What is deliberately left out. Dividends are excluded from every column, which understates all three results and understates day-one investing most, because it is in the market longest. No tax is charged on the waiting interest. No fund fee, bid-offer spread, Treasury bill purchase friction or advisory fee is charged. The indexes are not the portfolio an heir would actually hold. The 1929 equity series is a 30-stock price average that changed composition, and the 2000, 2008 and 2020 series is the Nasdaq Composite, a technology-weighted index.
  5. Not a backtest of this decision. These episodes are history, not a forecast, and a future inheritance will not arrive on a 1929 or a 2008 schedule. The point is to size the entry risk, not to predict it.

Synthetic stress case, kept for comparison

What this test does and does not say

It tests the requested choice: after a hypothetical 2045 inheritance and sale, keep proceeds in short-dated Treasuries for 24 or 36 months, then move equal monthly portions into equities over the following 12 months. It compares that path with investing immediately.

Two tests run side by side. The synthetic stress assumes a stylized crash at entry (a -40% equity year, then +25%, then +10%, then the baseline 6.3% net nominal equity return); it is a scenario, not history. The dated backtest applies the returns that actually followed the 1929, 2000 and 2008 entries. In 3 of 3 dated crash entries the phased path ended its first decade ahead of immediate entry; in the synthetic no-shock case, phasing costs between −$3,337,360 and −$4,532,990 of terminal value. Phasing is insurance whose premium is the no-crash opportunity cost, not a free lunch. The 36-month crash row equals its no-crash row because the synthetic crash occupies the first three years and a 36-month park buys no equity until after that window, so the shock never touches the equity balance; that identity is the longer park working as intended, not a copying error.

The parking yield is the model's one canonical 3-month-to-2-year Treasury parking scenario (4.2%, band 3.5%-5.0%), stated, not a forecast. This backtest uses annual total returns so it can reach the 1929 entry and count dividends; the monthly replay further down this page re-runs the 2000, 2008 and 2020 entry months with official FRED monthly data (a price index without dividends), so the two tables test the same idea at two granularities and two data sources. The actual bill/note yield, reinvestment rates, taxes on interest, transaction costs and the chosen equity allocation must be refreshed with a fiduciary before implementation.

Interpretation and reinvestment risk

  • Waiting reduces the dollars exposed to an immediate crash, but it does not eliminate market risk: equity purchases still face subsequent losses.
  • With no crash, waiting has an opportunity cost if equities outperform the parking yield. The no-shock rows make that cost visible rather than showing only the favorable case.
  • Short Treasuries mature frequently. If market yields are lower at maturity, proceeds must be reinvested at lower rates; if rates rise, existing notes' market value can fall before maturity. Neither future yield nor ladder is known today.
  • Every phased figure on this site comes from one canonical monthly engine and one parking-yield assumption - the same engine behind the dashboard's phased headline path.
  • All terminal values omit estate-level investment tax details, a personalized asset allocation, withdrawals, fees beyond the model's stated drag, and changes in future law. Ask the CPA and investment fiduciary to rerun with an actual sale estimate and current Treasury quotes.

What the stress cases say

Selling and investing wins in 4 of the 6 regimes. Keeping the house wins only in: Great Inflation repeat; A fast inflation burst then a rate shock. Where house prices grew at an exceptional pace, keeping the house can win; that is a bet on one local market outgrowing the state-wide record, and the historical record says that sometimes it does and mostly it does not.

Two findings survive every regime. First, the property tax bill after reassessment is large and completely insensitive to how the economy performs: assessed value rises at the 2% cap whether house prices boom or crash. Second, letting the property out from the start is the lowest-valued path in 3 of the 6 regimes, because it pays the full reassessment and the running costs, and then competes with a diversified portfolio on yield.
