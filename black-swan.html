What smooth projections miss

The official baseline, the recorded extremes since 1974, and the same decision re-run through the worst decades on record. Growth and inflation are never smooth over fifty years.

What the stresses say

  1. Selling and reinvesting finishes first in 7 of 7 cases. The sale happens at the inherited value on day one, so no path of prices afterwards can hurt it. Keeping the home never finishes first in any of these cases.
  2. The vehicle inside the sale depends on the interest-rate regime. The stock fund wins 3 cases at the baseline and low-rate stresses; Treasuries win 4: three at the 1981 yield of 13.91%, and one when equities replay their worst decade. At that yield, after tax, nothing else comes close.
  3. The equity lost decade is the one case that comes close. Replaying the real 2000-2010 path for the stock fund, the fund ends $5,713,820 behind Treasuries and renting ends $436,120 behind them. A single lost decade is the strongest argument for splitting the proceeds between the two vehicles rather than picking one.
  4. Housing stress hits whoever keeps the home. On the real 2006 path renting finishes $3,305,310 behind selling, because the terminal sale happens after a decade with almost no gain.
  5. Insurance at three times the guess changes nothing. The ranking holds, as it does on the decision page. The regulator's own words, a crisis of availability, are the reason the premium is still a flagged item rather than a number.
  6. Moving in never wins on money. It stays a lifestyle choice, priced at about $8,580 a month in today's dollars in the baseline. Stress does not change that; it widens the gap.

Limits of these tests: each path is one real decade, not fifty years; the equity path is a price index with no dividends, which understates the stock fund; the metro index stands in for, but is not, the local market; and the combined case is constructed from shocks that did not occur together.
