Black-swan conditions

Growth and inflation are never smooth

Fifty years of official data, the baseline it contradicts, and how the plan holds up when it happens again.

Black swans, part 1 of 2 — conditions and history. What the baseline assumes, fifty years of official inflation data, and the amounts that do not rise with inflation. Part 2 re-runs the inheritance decision against verified episodes: stress tests.

Why this page exists

The rest of this site projects one smooth path: house prices at 3%, 4% or 5% a year, costs at 2.5%, stocks at 7%. That is a baseline, not a forecast. Over any fifty-year period, growth and inflation are never smooth. This page records (1) what the baseline assumes and which official body agrees with it, (2) what actually happened over the last fifty years on official data, and (3) how the plan responds if it happens again. Nothing here changes the recommendation; the stress cases show how much would have to change before it moved.

Fifty years of official inflation data

The state board publishes the consumer-price factor used to cap assessed-value increases. It is the longest official inflation series this site relies on, and it is not smooth: the index rose more than 6% a year from 1976-77 through 1982-83, peaked at 17.316% in 1980-81, fell to minus 0.237% in 2010-11, and ran at 7.274% as recently as 2023-24. The 2% cap on assessed values bound in 40 of the 51 assessment years from 1976-77 to 2026-27 (counted from the official factor table). Note also the gap in official statistics: because of the federal lapse in appropriations in autumn 2025, October 2025 prices were never collected, so the 2026-27 factor was calculated from August 2024 to August 2025 instead.

The pattern across the whole table: selling soon after the inheritance stays the first choice in every stress case tested. What changes is what you sell into. A lost decade for stocks (4.0% a year) moves the money from the stock fund to Treasuries, not back into the rental. The only thing that reverses the ranking is a set of conditions together, not any single shock: see the break-even points on the decision page. The independent cross-check edition reaches the same conclusion from official figures alone.

What is not modelled

  • Sequence risk inside the ten years. The model applies one average return a year. A crash in year one and a boom in year nine produce the same average but a different experience; Black Monday and 2008 show the size of single-year moves.
  • Insurance premiums. No official premium or quote could be confirmed; tripling the owner's guess does not change the ranking, and that test is on the decision page.
  • Disasters. No flood, fire or earthquake risk premium is modelled for either property; insurance is flagged until quotes exist.
  • Any debt on the properties. The model assumes both properties are unencumbered; the records supplied do not say. Flagged for the owner.

Assessment-roll values and tax bills are published at the precision the owner chose: roll values to the nearest $10. That is the owner's instruction of 12 November 2026. Rounding to $10 protects against a search for an exact figure, but a roll value that close to the assessed roll, together with a 2% growth chain going back to 2014, can still be matched to a parcel by someone who is looking. The stronger protection is tier 2 (nearest $1,000) or tier 3 (nearest $10,000); the privacy choice sets out the trade-off and how to change it. Every other figure is rounded to the nearest $10.
