Why this is a benchmark and not this property's bill

Two things have to be true before any of those figures apply to this property: that this is the supplier the home is on, and that the meter is the size assumed above. The bill settles both. The county also has water suppliers that are not regulated by the state commission and publish their own rates, so the tariff above may not be the one that applies.

What it does establish is that the owner's estimate of $1,200 to $1,800 a year is plausible: the published tariff puts a typical household close to that range. It does not verify the estimate. The sewer charge is a separate line, and for Property A it is not published at all: the district that serves this parcel has not been identified, so there is no schedule to cite, and the amount is left blank and flagged. The section below sets out what a published sewer schedule looks like and where this property's own rates would be found.

The cost of citing it. The linked tariff sheet describes the supplier's service area, so opening it narrows the possible location of the property. Why no sewer figure is published for Property A

Every figure in that table comes from the schedule of the city Property B is in. Property A is in a different county, on a different utility, and served by a sewer district that has not been identified, so there is no schedule this site can cite for it and no amount is inferred. The charge is left blank and flagged, and it is read from the annual property tax bill instead.

Two things are settled and worth knowing. First, in the county a sewer entity may elect to have its charge collected on the tax roll with the general taxes, and it must then file the parcel-by-parcel charge with the county every year; the election stays in force until the rates change or it is repealed. A county sewer charge therefore usually arrives as a line on the annual tax bill rather than a monthly utility bill. Second, the state publishes a map of the tax rate areas for the county and the county's assessor can identify the area, and the agencies, that apply to a parcel, which is how the district and the parcel's own rate would be found.

What an insurance benchmark does and does not tell you

The California Department of Insurance publishes average written premiums from its Residential Property Insurance Report. The statewide average for an owner-occupied homeowners policy was about $1,070 in experience year 2017. Both properties here are carried at $1,500 a year, which is an owner estimate and is flagged unverified.

The benchmark is statewide and across all amounts of insurance. It is not a premium for either building, and it must not be read as one: a three-unit rental in the city and a single-family home in the county are priced on entirely different bases. It is published here only so the owner estimate can be seen against something official, which is the most this source can support. A property-specific premium remains blank until it is quoted.

Three cost numbers that need replacing

  1. Insurance on both properties. The California Department of Insurance does not publish an average premium by area, so no official figure exists to check these against. Get three quotes for each building, one of which should be a rental policy. See suggestion S-01.
  2. Maintenance. No figure was supplied. This site assumes 0.5% of value a year for an owner-occupied home, 0.75% for Property A as a rental and 1.5% for the older three-unit building. Replace these with the actual five-year repair history.
  3. Water. The provider and tariff for Property A are not confirmed, so the owner's range is used as supplied.

Monetary results are rounded to the nearest $10 before they are printed.
