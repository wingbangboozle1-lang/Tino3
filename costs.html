What an insurance benchmark does and does not tell you

Two official series exist. The NAIC Homeowners Insurance Report (2023 data, published July 2026) is the state-level statistical series regulators use: average premium is written premium divided by written exposures in house-years, as reported to statistical agents, for the dwelling fire and homeowners policy forms separately. National Association of Insurance Commissioners The California Department of Insurance publishes its own statewide average written premium for each policy form, and the newest year of the series read for this site, experience year 2017, gives $1,074 for an owner-occupied homeowners multi-peril policy California Department of Insurance and $519 for the form the Department describes as a rental dwelling or landlord policy on up to four units California Department of Insurance - the form family a three-unit building like Property B is written on. The owner-occupied average for that form has itself risen from $620 in 2001, so a statewide average is a moving thing as well as a blended one. Until this edition neither building's insurance was compared with anything: both were carried at a single $1,500 figure, which is the middle of a band now rather than the whole assumption.

Neither series is a premium for either building and neither must be read as one: a three-unit rental in the city is written on a dwelling fire form and a single-family home in the county on a homeowners form, at entirely different amounts of insurance. The NAIC report itself warns that average premium rises with the amount of insurance and that dwelling fire premiums are generally lower than homeowners package premiums because the coverage is more limited. A statewide average therefore understates a high-value home. The report's own by-state averages sit in Table 4 (page 30 onward) and Table 5 (page 135). Those pages were re-tried on 16 November 2026 and are still not machine-readable in this build, so no average-premium figure from them is published here, under the rule that nothing is inferred or copied second-hand. A premium for either building remains blank until it is quoted: what the published form averages can do is bound an assumption, not replace it.

One coverage limit is worth the adviser's attention: the California FAIR Plan, the state's insurer of last resort, caps a residential dwelling policy at $3,000,000 combined (official CDI figure). Property A's owner-estimated value is $3,100,000, and that estimate is itself unverified. If standard-market cover cannot be placed for either building, cover above the cap would be needed and its cost is unknown until quoted. No premium for either building appears on this site; both remain owner estimates flagged unverified.

Insurance: the band, and what it is worth

Why this replaced a single number

The owner's brief asks for ranges in place of round-number assumptions. Insurance was the one running cost carried as a single figure at both properties - $1,500 a year each, the same number for a primary residence and for a three-unit rental, which cannot both be right. It is now a band, and each end says where it came from: the published statewide average for the matching policy form at the bottom, the owner's figure in the middle, and a stated upper scenario at the top.

The band matters less than it looks. Run through thirty years of the model, the whole spread from floor to ceiling moves the living path's terminal value by $265,200 against a selling-versus-living gap of $33,166,570 - about 0.8% of it. Insurance is therefore not a load-bearing assumption on this decision, and getting three quotes is about the size of the bill, not about which path to choose. The quotes still have to be obtained: they are what turns a band into a number.

The five-year maintenance and capital plan

A percentage of value is a budget, not a plan

The brief asks for a five-year maintenance and capital plan. The model has maintenance as 0.50% of value a year for an owner-occupied home and 1.50% for the three-unit building, growing with inflation. That is the right shape for a thirty-year projection and the wrong shape for planning five years of work, because a roof is not spread evenly. Below, the same money is written as a five-year budget, and the components it has to cover are listed with their typical intervals. Nothing in the component list is priced: nothing has been quoted, so a figure would be invented.

A house costs more to keep when it is let: turnover, longer hours of use and a landlord's obligations. Moving from 0.50% to 0.75% of value is the assumption this site makes, and it is unverified. If it is too low, the letting path is flattered; if it is too high, occupying and then letting looks worse than it is.

Three cost numbers that need replacing

  1. Insurance on both properties. This was one round number at each building - the same number for a primary residence and for a three-unit rental, which cannot both be right - and it is now a band, floored by the Department's published statewide average for the matching policy form and capped by a stated scenario: see Insurance: the band, and what it is worth. The industry benchmark is the NAIC Homeowners Insurance Report, whose by-state averages for the correct forms sit in Table 4, beyond the pages this build can read, so they are left blank rather than copied from a secondary source. No official source publishes a premium for this postal area or for either building. Get three quotes for each building, one of which should be a landlord policy. See suggestion S-01.
  2. Maintenance. No figure was supplied. This site assumes 0.5% of value a year for an owner-occupied home, 0.75% for Property A as a rental and 1.5% for the older three-unit building. Replace these with the actual five-year repair history. The five-year capital plan above writes the same allowance out year by year and lists the components it has to cover.
  3. Water. The provider and tariff for Property A are not confirmed, so the owner's range is used as supplied.

How the numbers on this page are produced

Every computed figure on this page comes from scripts/model.py, which reads assets/data/inputs.json and assets/data/series.json. Monetary results are rounded to the nearest $10 before they are printed. The rules the model implements are the published official rules below; where a page uses a stated assumption or an unverified owner figure, that row says so and is logged on the register of unverified and assumed items.
